# The Far Side of the Horizon: what a CPT fold does at a black hole, and what it does not

*Linnet Labs, Sydney, Australia*

*Companion to* Separate Ways and the Upside Down: a CPT fold, a ceiling on the dark matter, and

## Abstract

A CPT-folded universe relates the two sides of a horizon by an antilinear involution. This paper
asks what that does at a black hole, and answers it mostly in the negative, which is the useful
part. Three one-copy readings of the far side are tested and fail on stated grounds: a constant
image amplitude cannot satisfy both positivity and the Hadamard condition in the conformal-scalar
ansatz, the proposed entropy-temperature pairing fails the first law, and a complete reciprocal
time-symmetric response predicts zero binary damping. None of that excludes every quotient theory,
and the paper says where the tests stop.

What the fold then supplies at a horizon is one map and not a theorem. A reciprocal identity
relates the fold's cross-leg kernel to the symmetric thermal contour's; it is algebraic at every
separation and carries no temperature, so the work is done by a separate match that returns the
Gibbons-Hawking value at the primitive KMS period. The seam action's one free coefficient is
**adopted** as transparent, not derived. Two arguments that would have derived it are withdrawn
here with the calculations that kill them: one is circular, and one is empty because the image
term and the singularity it forbids never occupy the same region. A reflecting alternative remains
open and owes a matching law nobody has constructed.

The companion cosmology paper carries the dark-matter mass ceiling, the neutrino line and the mass
floor. None of those results depend on anything here.


## When the time directions lose coherence

The real Hartle-Hawking wavefunction contains expanding and contracting WKB
branches [20]. Inhomogeneous modes can decohere them, as in the work of
Halliwell, Hawking and Kiefer [17–19,45]. Here the two branches are compared by an
explicit overlap. The closed-de Sitter throat and the radiation bang are
different calculations and use different clocks.

The closed-de Sitter calculation uses standard no-boundary branch states and
does not require BFT's matter model. Identifying those branches with the
CPT-related copies of §2 is an additional step, and it is worth being exact about
what supports it and what does not, though the paper's §3 numbers do not depend on it: that section adopts its cosmology as a separate input and uses none of the patch algebra.

What supports it is that one map does both jobs. In the embedding, $\alpha$ sends
$X\mapsto-X$, so it carries the right static patch $X_1>|X_0|$ to the left one and
simultaneously sends $X_0\mapsto-X_0$, hence $t\mapsto-t$, exchanging the
contracting and expanding phases of $a(t)=\cosh Ht/H$. Both exchanges are the same
involution acting on two coordinates at once, so the assignment of the right patch
and the expanding branch to one sheet, and the left patch and the contracting
branch to the other, requires no second map and makes no choice twice.

One further thing follows, and one does not. What relates the two patch algebras
is an antiunitary, conjugation composed with a geometric map. What relates the two
branch states is also conjugation: the closed-de Sitter overlap below obtains them as complex conjugates because
the Wheeler-DeWitt operator is real. Both are conjugation, and $\alpha$ performs it. Its time reversal sends $\eta\to-\eta$, and the environment's
frequency function obeys $\Omega_n(-\eta)=\overline{\Omega_n(\eta)}$.

That identity is algebraically empty on its own and we do not offer it as
evidence: every function of the form (even $+\,i\,$odd)$/$even satisfies it. What
is not empty is that $\Omega_n$ has that form. Its real part carries the $\eta$ dependence only through an even denominator and its
imaginary part is odd, and the reason is narrower than the reality of the
Wheeler-DeWitt operator, which alone gives only the empty conjugate relation. What
produces the form is a real equation together with a real datum at $\eta=0$:
$\Omega_n(0)=(n^2-1)/n$ is real because of the no-boundary condition at the throat,
and $a(\eta)=1/(H\cos\eta)$ is even there. Given the form, the Gaussian
$\psi_n\propto\exp(-\Omega_n\chi^2/2)$ on one branch is the antilinear image of the
other's.

That the no-boundary condition holds exactly is checkable and worth stating,
because it settles what kind of object these states are: $\Omega_n(0)=(n^2-1)/n$ is
real at every $n$ with the Wronskian normalised to one, which is the Euclidean
condition, and the Euclidean state on closed de Sitter is Bunch-Davies. The two
branch states are therefore the same Bunch-Davies state conditioned on the two
branches, not states on some other space, and its restriction to a static patch is
KMS at $T=H/2\pi$ by Gibbons-Hawking, which A.6 already uses.

What is still not shown is the identification itself, and the obstruction is
specific. The two pairs are different partitions of the same manifold: the patch
pair is $X_1>|X_0|$ and its image, the branch pair is $X_0>0$ and $X_0<0$, and the
right static patch meets both branches. One involution exchanging the members of
each does not make them one pair. Conditioning a state on a WKB branch is also not the same operation as restricting
it to a region, and we have now done the calculation that was to settle it. It
settles it in the negative, and cleanly enough to be worth stating as a result
rather than a caveat.

The right static patch is the causal diamond of a worldline and reaches past and
future infinity: solving $X_1^2=H^{-2}+X_0^2-(X_2^2+X_3^2+X_4^2)$, the patch
condition $X_1>|X_0|$ bounds only the transverse part, so the patch contains points
at every global time. The branch label is $\mathrm{sign}(\dot a)$, hence
$\mathrm{sign}(X_0)$, and the patch straddles both signs in equal measure. An
operator localised in the patch therefore has support on both branches, and no
element of the patch algebra determines the branch label.

So the branch conditioning is not an operation on the patch algebra at all. It acts
on the gravitational wavefunction, whose split is by global time; the restriction
acts on the matter algebra, whose split is by causal position. The two do not fail
to commute so much as fail to compose, and the identification cannot be made this
way.

What survives is exact and is what we claim. There is one state, the Bunch-Davies
state, and it carries two splittings: by causal position into the two patches, and
by global time into the two branches. $\alpha$ exchanges the members of each. The
patch restriction is KMS at $T=H/2\pi$ and the branch conditioning returns complex
conjugates. Those are true statements about a single state with two splittings that
one involution relates, and the splittings cross rather than coincide. The horizon results of §§2 and 3 use the first splitting, and the
paper's cosmological results use neither, taking their inputs from the adopted cosmology instead, so nothing below
rests on an identification we have just shown cannot be made. The contracting branch's environment state is not merely exchanged with
the expanding one by $\alpha$; it *is* $\alpha$'s image of it. The two-sheet
structure is therefore one structure appearing in two sectors rather than two
structures asserted to match, and no map from a region to a solution is required.

The limit is the setting. This is the free nonconformal environment of the closed-de Sitter overlap below on
closed de Sitter, and the check is that the antilinear lift acts as conjugation on
that environment's frequency function. It has not been carried to the interacting
matter sector at a radiation bang, which is where the closed-de Sitter overlap below, and the paper's §3
numbers, live, and
where the same argument would need the corresponding mode functions. The step is
therefore exhibited on the free de Sitter environment and open at the bang, which
is a smaller gap than the one it replaces and a specific one.

### A closed-de Sitter overlap

Closed FRW minisuperspace with $\Lambda=3H^2$ has a classically allowed region
beginning at the no-boundary throat $aH=1$. For $N_f$ minimally coupled
massless scalar fields, the exact mode function gives

$$
\Omega_n=
\frac{n(n^2-1)-i\tan\eta\,\sec^2\eta}{\tan^2\eta+n^2},
\qquad
\operatorname{Im}\Omega_n=
-\frac{\dot a\,(aH)^2}{\dot a^2+n^2}.
$$

The two branches carry complex-conjugate Gaussian environment states. With
$A\equiv aH$, their overlap is

$$
\log|\langle E_-|E_+\rangle|
=-\frac{N_f}{4}\sum_{n\ge2}n^2
\log\!\left[
1+\frac{A^4(A^2-1)}{n^2(n^2-1)^2}
\right],
$$

with large-$A$ asymptote
$-(\pi/12)N_fA^3$. Appendix A.8 derives the sum. Summed over all harmonics, for one
minimally coupled scalar, the overlap reaches $e^{-1}$ at $aH=1.95374$, $Ht=1.28983$.

That is the ungauged number, and the fold does not leave it standing. Section 2.4 adopts Harlow and
Numasawa's argument that a spacetime inversion cannot be a global symmetry and must be gauged [39].
The fold has a linear involution available to gauge, and it is not $\Theta$: $\Theta$ is antilinear
and so is the modular conjugation, so their composition is linear and implements $P_\perp$. Gauging
it restricts physical states to its invariant sector. Care is needed over which involution acts.
$P_\perp$ is $\mathrm{diag}(+1,+1,-1,-1,-1)$ in the embedding, so on a closed slice it sends
$(X_1,X_2,X_3,X_4)\mapsto(X_1,-X_2,-X_3,-X_4)$: it fixes one spatial direction and reverses three,
and is not the antipodal map of $S^3$, which reverses all four. It therefore does not multiply a
harmonic level by a sign, it splits it. Degree-$k$ harmonics decompose under the $SO(3)$ acting on
$(X_2,X_3,X_4)$ into $l=0,\dots,k$, and $P_\perp$ acts on the $l$ multiplet as $(-1)^l$, so the
invariant dimension at level $n=k+1$ is $\sum_{l\ \mathrm{even}}(2l+1)$ rather than $n^2$ or
nothing. At $n=2$ the four harmonics split as one invariant and three not. That is the correct
one-particle decomposition and it is not the mode count the overlap uses. Restricting physical
states removes no oscillator from the product. The branch states are Gaussian in each mode
coordinate, $\psi_\pm(q)\propto e^{-(a\pm ib)q^2/2}$, and a Gaussian in $q^2$ cannot tell $+q$ from
$-q$, so the projector $(1+U(P_\perp))/2$ acts on it as the identity whatever the parity of the
harmonic it belongs to. Each mode keeps its overlap factor $(1+b^2/a^2)^{-1/4}<1$ instead of having
it replaced by one. Two quanta in an odd
mode are invariant as well.

So the threshold is the ungauged one,

$$
aH=1.95376,\qquad Ht=1.28984,
$$

recomputed independently from the displayed sum.
Two superseded figures are on the record here rather than removed. This section previously
reported $aH=2.43913$ and $Ht=1.53984$, a threshold 19 per cent later, by deleting the
odd-parity oscillators; that delay is withdrawn, since deleting them needs a restriction on the
field rather than on the states, which is a stronger hypothesis than the one §2.4 adopts and one
this paper does not derive. Before that it reported $2.59538$, obtained by keeping whole levels
of odd $n$, which is the parity of the full antipodal map rather than of $P_\perp$. Nothing has to be added back: gauging a
discrete group introduces sectors classified by $H^1(M;\mathbb Z_2)$, and A.3
establishes that this vanishes on the cover, so there is one bundle, no twisted
sector, and the invariant subspace is the whole physical space. A.3 records that
vanishing as a negative, against a holonomy route to the stabilising rule; here it
is what makes the gauged answer clean. Recorded for whoever revisits the withdrawn restriction: the ratio of restricted to
unrestricted exponent tends to one half at large $A$, and both ways of dropping modes approach
it, 0.4999 and 0.4998 at $A=40$, so that limit never distinguished which half had been dropped
and would not have caught the error either.

The exponent contains no explicit $\hbar$ or $G$; the background Hubble rate
sets the time scale. The threshold is a declared measure of decoherence, not a
discontinuous creation of time or a rule selecting one branch. Conformally
coupled free fields have real widths in this calculation and do not contribute
to the separation. The result changes with the environment and with the
chosen overlap threshold.

The $RP^3$ harmonic restriction is a separate topology-dependent comparison, and
the distinction from the paragraph above is worth stating, because the two are
easily run together. The cover does not acquire a restriction merely because its
fields carry an antilinear CPT relation; an antilinear relation between two sheets
restricts nothing on either. What restricts is the linear involution inside it,
once gauged, and that has nothing to do with the topology of the quotient. One
restriction comes of choosing a different spacetime, the other follows from a
symmetry this paper already commits to. The numerical comparison with $RP^3$
is given in the supplement.


**A.6 A worked instance of the in-in dictionary.** Take the massless conformally coupled scalar on
dS$_4$ and put the observer on the central worldline, *r* = 0. There the transverse sphere degenerates,
the transverse parity acts trivially, and *A* = *J* exactly, so the fold's image kernel and the thermal
contour's cross-leg propagator are the same function of time:

> $$
> G_{\mathrm{img}}(t)=\frac{H^2}{16\pi^2}\operatorname{sech}^2\!\left(\frac{Ht}{2}\right)
> =W(t-i\beta/2)=G_{+-}^{\sigma=\beta/2}(t),
> \qquad \beta=\frac{2\pi}{H}.
> $$

The three expressions agree in the stated Bunch-Davies conformal-scalar example. Define the analytic
half-period continuation $F_{\rm half}(t)=F_W(t-i\beta/2)$. With the convention
$F(E)=\int dt\,e^{-iEt}F(t)$, strip analyticity gives
$F_{\rm half}(E)=e^{\beta E/2}F_W(E)$. Identifying this analytic continuation with a
geometric image $W(x,\alpha y)$ additionally requires the state's geometric reflection and parity
implementation, including index transport for fields with spin; KMS alone does not establish that
identification for every state, mass or spin. With the
spectral density $\rho$(*E*) = *E*/4$\pi ^2$, fixed by the Gibbons–Hawking detector response,
the reconstruction $\int_0^\infty dE\,\rho(E)\cos(Et)/\sinh(\beta E/2)$ returns the same
sech$^2$, and a five-energy numerical check reproduces
$F_{\rm img}=e^{\beta E/2}F_{\rm BD}$. These are implementation checks of the stated analytic conformal-scalar identities, not physical-precision claims. Off the central worldline the two maps part company. For two points at radii $r_1$ and $r_2$
subtending an angle $\gamma$ at the centre, with time separation $\Delta t$, the embedding
invariants differ by

$$
Z_\alpha-Z_J=-2r_1r_2\cos\gamma,
$$

independent of $\Delta t$, which reduces to $-2r^2$ at equal radii and $\gamma=0$. A relative
discrepancy needs a denominator, and the two choices behave differently. Referred to the fold
kernel it is $r^2/[(1-r^2)\cosh^2(\Delta t/2)]$, which is 9.9% at $r=0.3$ and 178% at $r=0.8$ for
$\Delta t=0$; referred to the thermal kernel it is $-r^2$, bounded by unity. The two differ by
$(1-Z_J)/(1-Z_\alpha)$ and that factor carries the apparent growth.

The invariant statement is the ratio. At $\Delta t=0$ and $\gamma=0$ the invariants are
$Z_J=-1+2r^2$ and $Z_\alpha=-1$, so $1-Z_\alpha=2$ identically and

$$
G_\alpha=\tfrac12,\qquad G_J=\frac{1}{2(1-r^2)},\qquad G_\alpha/G_J=1-r^2,
$$

in units where the prefactor is dropped. The fold's image kernel is constant in radius. The
thermal contour's cross-leg kernel is the one that diverges at the de Sitter horizon, and at
$r=1/\sqrt2$ the fold kernel is half of it. The transverse parity flips the sign of the $r^2$ term, which is what cancels the radial dependence. This is a statement about $\gamma=0$ and does not generalise as it stands. At the horizon $1-Z_J\to1-\cos\gamma$ and $1-Z_\alpha\to1+\cos\gamma$, so each kernel is singular exactly where its own map brings the two points together: the thermal contour's at coincident directions, the fold's at antipodal ones. Neither is the better behaved overall. What is general is the horizon ratio,

$$
G_\alpha/G_J\to\tan^2(\gamma/2),
$$

independent of everything else and reproduced to $4\times10^{-7}$. Written with unit direction vectors this is

$$
\frac{G_\alpha}{G_J}\to\frac{|\hat n_1-\hat n_2|^2}{|\hat n_1-(-\hat n_2)|^2},
$$

the squared chord between the two points over the squared chord from one to the other's antipode. $J$ leaves the transverse direction alone, while $\alpha$ carries it to the antipodal one, so each kernel is controlled by the separation of its own pair of arguments. The three features §2.2 states then need no calculation here: each is the ordinary short-distance singularity of a two-point function, appearing at the angle its own identification selects. The $1-Z_\alpha=2$ identity is specific to $\gamma=0$ and $\Delta t=0$; the $\tan^2(\gamma/2)$ ratio holds at every angle. Both are statements about the conformally coupled massless scalar in the Bunch-Davies state, and nothing is claimed for spin two or for other states. The $\cosh^{-2}$ confines the effect to coincident
times, decaying as $4e^{-|\Delta t|}$ once $|\Delta t|\gg1$.

The angular dependence needs care. The invariant difference is exactly $P_1(\cos\gamma)$, but the
discrepancy is not, because $\cos\gamma$ also enters its denominator. Writing $b=r_1r_2$ and $a=1+\sqrt{(1-r_1^2)(1-r_2^2)}\cosh\Delta t$, the fold-referred discrepancy $-2b\cos\gamma/(a-b\cos\gamma)$ expands as $-(2b/a)\sum_{n\ge0}(b/a)^n\cos^{n+1}\gamma$, with no alternation because the denominator carries $-b\cos\gamma$; the thermal-referred form $-2b\cos\gamma/(a+b\cos\gamma)$ gives the same series with $(-1)^n$ inserted, so every multipole appears, suppressed by powers of
$b/a$, which at equal radii and $\Delta t=0$ is $r^2/(2-r^2)$. The dipole dominates only while
that is small: the quadrupole is 3.1% of it at $r=0.3$, 23% at $r=1/\sqrt2$ and 53% at $r=0.9$.
What holds exactly at every radius and every time separation is the angular zero, which the
numerator alone carries: the discrepancy vanishes at $\gamma=\pi/2$ and reverses sign across it
whatever the higher multipoles do. That zero does not weaken with radius and is the sharp feature. The
multipole contamination is a property of the variable and not of the fold. The discrepancy
is a difference of kernels; the ratio is not, and by the reciprocal law of A.10
$\log(G_\alpha/G_J)$ is odd about $\gamma=\pi/2$ for any Hadamard state, so its even
multipoles vanish *identically* at every radius and every time separation, rather than
only while $b/a$ is small. That is algebra and not a measurement: a projection at
$r=0.9$, where the quadrupole of the discrepancy is 53% of its dipole, returns even
coefficients below $10^{-15}$ for four unrelated profiles, which tests the quadrature
and not the claim. What a reader should want
instead is the truncation error, since the logarithmic endpoints make the series
converge in $L^2$ but not pointwise there, and a truncated reconstruction rings within
$O(1/\ell_{\max})$ of $\gamma=0$ and $\pi$. The dipole dominance that has to be argued
for in the difference is exact in the logarithm, away from the two endpoints.

Two readings have to be excluded. The discrepancy depends on $r_1$, $r_2$, $\gamma$ and $\Delta t$
alone, so it is invariant under a rigid rotation of both points and selects no axis; $P_\perp$ is
a parity and parity commutes with rotations. The fold cannot generate a directional dipole of the
kind reported in radio and quasar number counts, at any amplitude. Neither is this a correction to
ordinary clustering: the objects compared are the cross-leg kernels $G_{12}$ of 2.2, while the
galaxy two-point function is built from the same-leg propagator. Read as a fractional correction
to $w(\gamma)$ in a narrow redshift slice it would give tens of per cent by $z\simeq1$ and more
than unity by $z\simeq2$, which observed clustering excludes outright, and the identification
rather than the fold is what that rules out. The cross-leg kernel enters the noise and dissipation
sector, which is where the force-noise ports below place it.

For scale, $r$ is the areal radius in units of the de Sitter radius, so in the flat slicing it is
physical distance in horizon radii. Taking $H_\Lambda=H_0\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}$ and a
$\Lambda$CDM distance-redshift relation for orientation only, $r=0.3$ is $z\simeq0.40$, $r=0.8$ is
$z\simeq1.4$, and the horizon $r=1$ is $z\simeq2.0$. The largest angular scales of the microwave
background lie well outside this range: a $60^\circ$ separation on the last-scattering surface
corresponds to $r\simeq2.6$, so nothing in this comparison bears on the large-angle correlation anomalies. The future-horizon quadrupole conjecture of Supplement S7 reaches them by a different route and at a different scale, and the two are independent.

The same structure governs a quantity that is not a consistency check. For a comoving scalar
charge the local and image force-noise kernels form a two-port matrix with fold-even and fold-odd
eigenvalues $A_0\pm A_I$. On the central worldline the image kernel is exactly minus the
half-period shift of the local one, so $A_I=-A_0=-1/12\pi^2$ and the even port vanishes
identically. The $-2r^2$ breaks that identity. Carrying the discrepancy into the kernel and
integrating, with $\int dt\,{\rm sech}^6(t/2)=32/15$, gives an even-port lift against an unchanged
odd port $1/6\pi^2$. Referred to the fold kernel the lift is $-r^2/[15\pi^2(1-r^2)]$; referred to
the thermal kernel it saturates, approaching $-1/12\pi^2$ as $r\to1$, exactly half the odd port.
Under that thermal referencing the even channel is lifted off zero at every $r>0$ and stays the smaller of the two at all radii; referred to the fold kernel it instead reaches the odd port at $r=\sqrt{5/7}$, which is the same normalisation artefact again rather than a physical crossover. The degeneracy is exact only on axis, the misalignment enters at second order, and the
sign is fixed. Carrying a propagator-level discrepancy into the force-noise kernel is a
leading-order identification rather than a spin-2 derivation; the lifting of the zero and its
order in $r$ do not depend on that step, the coefficients do.

## 3. Tests of states and responses

Section 2.1 left three readings sharing one operator: quotient spacetime by it,
impose a reality condition on the quotient, or keep both sheets and pair the
contour legs. They are not equivalent, and the tests below separate them from
three unrelated directions. The first is algebraic, on which states the operator
admits at all; the second is thermodynamic, on the first law; the third is
dynamical, against a binary-pulsar measurement. Nothing connects the three
arguments except the reading they select.

Each names the ansatz or response law it tests, and a failure constrains that
choice rather than every possible quotient. The scope matters and is kept
throughout. What it does not diminish is that three independent lines of attack,
from three different parts of physics, fall the same way.

### 3.1 The constant image-state ansatz

For the de Sitter-invariant conformally coupled scalar ansatz

$$
W=\tfrac12 i\Delta+A\,G_{\rm sym}(Z)+B\,G(-Z),
$$

positivity requires $A\geq1$ and $A^2-B^2\geq1$. Hadamard normalisation at coincidence
requires $A=1$, and therefore $B=0$. A nonzero constant image amplitude cannot
satisfy both conditions. Positive image states outside the Hadamard class,
including the alpha-vacuum family, are not excluded by positivity alone [23].
The result also leaves open smooth, state-dependent finite-frequency
correlations outside this two-amplitude ansatz. Appendix A.7 gives the mode
covariance and the two inequalities.

### 3.2 The entropy-temperature pairing

The proposed elliptic pairing $(T_H,\mathcal A/8G)$, where $\mathcal A$ is the
cover horizon area, fails the Smarr relation $M=2TS$ and the first law
$dM=T\,dS$. For example, in Schwarzschild units $G=M=1$,
$T_H=1/(8\pi)$ and $\mathcal A=16\pi$: the proposed pair gives $2TS=1/2$
and $T_Hd(\mathcal A/8G)=dM/2$. The two-copy pairing
$(T_H,\mathcal A/4G)$ and 't Hooft's pairing
$(2T_H,\mathcal A/8G)$ both give $2TS=M$ and the correct first law. The quotient two-point function used in the comparison has imaginary period $\beta_H/2=2\pi R$ at the central worldline, half the Hawking period, so its even sector realizes the latter temperature. Away from the centre, all harmonics
contribute and no single temperature describes the response. Thermodynamics
therefore rejects the stated temperature-entropy assignment, not the quotient
geometry itself.

### 3.3 The complete reciprocal time-symmetric response

The antipode reverses time orientation and exchanges the retarded and advanced kernels. Within the affine family $\alpha G_{\rm ret}+(1-\alpha)G_{\rm adv}$, invariance selects

$$
G_{\rm sym}=\frac{G_{\rm ret}+G_{\rm adv}}{2}.
$$

For the stationary reciprocal problem considered here, this kernel is Hermitian. With a complete steady-state cycle, or vanishing all-history boundary terms, integration by parts gives zero total dissipative work. If this kernel is the binary's complete effective gravitational response, with no independent homogeneous drive or environmental contribution, the binary has no secular radiation damping. The observed orbital decay of PSR B1913+16 excludes that response: Weisberg and Huang report an observed-to-GR ratio of $0.9983\pm0.0016$ [21].

Quotient descent does not fix the complete response. An invariant homogeneous solution can change a lifted field without changing its source equation. Nor does antipodal parity imply local time parity: on $\mathbb R\times S^1$ with $A(t,\theta)=(-t,\theta+\pi)$, the homogeneous wave $h=\sin t\cos\theta$ is A-even, while $\partial_t h=\cos t\cos\theta$ is even in time at fixed position. A suitable local source can exchange work with this wave, with cancellation in the antipodal region. This scalar counterexample defeats the symmetry inference; it does not compute a gravitational self-force.

A twisted Green kernel is odd under the antipode in each argument and even under its simultaneous action on both arguments. Its identity distribution is the twisted delta. There is no contradiction of the form $\delta=-\delta$.

The surviving observational test is exact within its premises: a complete reciprocal time-symmetric response has no secular dissipative work and fails the pulsar measurement. A more general quotient response requires its own boundary and source problem. Friedman and Higuchi's locally defined quotient theory makes the distinction between local physics and global identification explicit [7].

### 3.4 The minimal black-hole response

For black holes, the minimal implementation retains ordinary Kerr perturbation dynamics and the absorbing horizon boundary condition. We add no seam interaction. At the order treated, it therefore predicts no additional horizon reflectivity and no fold-induced shift of the Kerr quasinormal spectrum.

The status of that boundary condition differs between two cases and the difference is worth
stating before it is used. Where the geometry is two-sided, which covers the cosmological
horizon and an eternal hole, A.13 *derives* it: a constant reflectivity puts an image term
between mirror-placed points that are spacelike separated, and no admissible state is
singular there. A hole formed by collapse has one exterior, so there are no mirror-placed
points and that argument does not run; for such a hole the absorbing condition is adopted,
as it is throughout the ringdown literature, rather than derived here. The prediction is the
same in both cases and only its standing changes, from a result to the standard choice.

The boundary condition is a physical commitment of this implementation. Flux conservation and covariance under the fold do not select it: the allowed seam matrices include a continuous range of reflectivities. That range is not narrowed by the remaining structure either. Symmetry under the fold and closure of the constraint algebra admit the whole family. Topology does not help either on the de Sitter cover, which is simply connected with $H^1$ vanishing for every coefficient group, so there are no periods to quantize a coefficient against (A.3).
That argument is about the cover, which is the wrong space for a gauged spacetime
involution, where sectors live on the quotient; the conclusion survives the correction and
the reasoning did not. $P_\perp$ acts on the closed slice as
$(X_1,X_2,X_3,X_4)\mapsto(X_1,-X_2,-X_3,-X_4)$, whose fixed set is $X_2=X_3=X_4=0$, two
points, so it is not free there even though it is free on $B$. Writing $X_1=\cos\theta$ and
the rest as $\sin\theta\,\hat n$, the map is $\hat n\mapsto-\hat n$ at fixed $\theta$, so
the quotient is the suspension of $\mathbb{RP}^2$. The suspension isomorphism then gives
$H^1=0$ there as well, which is what the argument needed. It also gives $H^2=\mathbb Z_2$, which the degree-one
argument never looked at; for the group $\mathbb Z_2$ itself $H^2(\mathbb Z_2;U(1))=0$, so
no discrete torsion arises from the group, and whether the space's $H^2$ does anything here
we leave open rather than assert either way; that argument belongs to $S^3\times\mathbb R$ and we do not carry it to a Kerr exterior. So no symmetry or topological argument on the content already present fixes the coefficient, and what would is either additional field content, of the kind a gauge holonomy or a compact scalar would supply, or a condition of a different type. A.13 supplies one of the second kind, Hadamard regularity, and it settles the corner term's coefficient without any further content at all. Those are all internal routes, and an external one fails too. What the failures
leave behind is more useful than a list of closures, because two of them are
requirements that any proposed seam law must meet, ours or anyone's.

The first is ultraviolet. A dimensionless coefficient in the corner term forces a
frequency-independent reflectivity, and no physical interface reflects identically at arbitrarily
short wavelength. A.13 tried two routes to that conclusion and keeps neither: asking for finite
relative entropy against the transparent case is circular, because the reference it compares against
is the seam it selects; and the image-kernel argument that replaced it is empty, because the image
term and the singularity it forbids never occupy the same region. Transparency is adopted there, not
derived, and A.13 says what would be needed to derive it. Completing a seam action in the
ultraviolet comes before fixing its coefficient for any seam that reflects. For the corner term the
question does not arise, because nothing here reflects: within the corner term $r$ cannot depend on
frequency, and the transparent member $\kappa=1$, $t=1$ is the one this paper adopts. A.13 records
that the boundary condition is a commitment chosen from a continuous range that flux conservation
and covariance leave open, and that two attempts to close that range by regularity have failed. The
requirement applies undiminished to the reflective proposals, which carry a scale the corner term
does not and therefore still owe their own matching law. A.13 also records what a seam carrying the
horizon's own scale would commit to, since that is the one way out, and it is not a free one.

The second is structural, and it says what a seam would have to be rather than what it cannot be.
Iyer-Wald entropy survives at a bifurcation surface because the Noether charge is built from the
Killing field and its derivative: the field vanishes there while the antisymmetrised derivative does
not. A connection inherits that surviving piece through the second term of its Lie derivative; a
matter field does not, since its Lie derivative is $\xi^a\partial_a$ of the field and dies with
$\xi$. Computed in Kruskal, the one-form's Lie derivative holds at $-\kappa_{\rm sg}A_U(0)$ at every
distance while the scalar's falls away as the surface is approached. So for the minimally coupled
scalar term examined here, that Lie-derivative contribution vanishes at the bifurcation surface. It
does not follow that any seam contributing to horizon entropy must be connection-valued: a
nonminimal coupling $f(\phi)R/16\pi G$ gives $S=(4G)^{-1}\int_B f(\phi)\,dA$ through the curvature
derivative in Wald's formula even when $\mathcal L_\xi\phi=0$, as Iyer and Wald's dilaton example
shows. The classification is withdrawn and the statement is restricted to the action studied. It
remains the reason the Chern-Simons precedents, which are connection-built, look close and do not
transfer. A dynamical matching law for a general rotating-horizon seam remains unconstructed here,
and the same requirement falls on any proposal with nonzero reflection, which must supply that law
before its waveform can be compared with the data.

Two constructions inside the CPT-symmetric programme bear on this commitment. Tzanavaris, Boyle and Turok's black mirror [34] identifies horizon points while keeping the exteriors distinct, a third reading: neither the one-copy quotient tested in 3.1 to 3.3 nor the two-sheet structure used here. Those tests target a quotient and do not bear on it. The difference that does is sharp. The black mirror makes the horizon a locus of identification; the fold identifies no points anywhere, and the fixed-point set of its map does not lie on the horizon. These are different boundary conditions on the same exterior dynamics, so they differ in the seam matching law, the horizon algebra and the entropy each supports. That is the law left unconstructed above. Constructing it decides between them; null results both readings survive do not.

Amaro Seoane [35] derives a horizon reflectivity for that model equal to the generalized Boltzmann
factor, fixed by $T_H$. Its reach is bounded by one thing. The constant-horizon condition gives
$\omega=0$ rather than a computed complex quasinormal spectrum, and the inspiral signature is
spin-dependent, so no universal sign for a damping-time shift follows. The second bound was
arithmetic and it was wrong. The generalized Boltzmann factor carries the co-rotation term,
$|R|=\exp[-|\omega-m\Omega_H|/2T_H]$, and dropping $m\Omega_H$ reverses the spin dependence. For the
fundamental $l=m=2$ mode, with the Berti-Cardoso-Will fit $M\omega=1.5251-1.1568(1-a)^{0.1292}$ this
paper already uses, the amplitude reflectivity rises with spin across the range that matters:
$9.8\times10^{-3}$ at $a=0$, where $\Omega_H$ vanishes and the two expressions agree, then $0.148$
at $a=0.7$ and $0.444$ at $a=0.9$, where the horizon returns about a fifth of the incident energy
rather than the $10^{-6}$ previously quoted. It is not monotone all the way to extremality on this
fit, which gives $0.662$ at $a=0.95$ and $0.372$ at $a=0.99$, so the rise is stated for the interval
computed and not extrapolated past it.

No exclusion follows. Under the most generous damping model, in which all ringdown damping is
horizon absorption and returning $|R|^2$ lengthens the damping time to $\tau/(1-|R|^2)$, reproducing
$\delta\hat\tau_{220}=0.14$ needs $|R|=0.35$. A reflectivity fixed by $T_H$ supplies that at
$a\gtrsim0.9$ with no extra heat at all: the horizon it asks for is $0.77\,T_H$ at $a=0.9$ and
$0.39\,T_H$ at $a=0.95$, cooler than Hawking rather than several times hotter. The earlier claim
that a $T_H$-fixed reflectivity could not reach the reported excess was an artefact of the dropped
co-rotation term. What survives is narrower and cuts both ways: a reflectivity magnitude is not a
quasinormal-mode calculation, so nothing here either excludes the black mirror or shows it accounts
for the excess. The gain is that the two readings now differ somewhere reachable. At low spin the
absorbing horizon adopted here returns nothing and a black mirror returns about $10^{-4}$, a gap no
instrument will reach; at $a\gtrsim0.9$ they differ by a fraction of order one. High-spin ringdown
is therefore where the structural difference between them becomes an observational one.

The published near-horizon scattering model of Betzios, Gaddam and Papadoulaki is a further concrete alternative [28]. Its small corrections are compatible with present echo bounds, so null searches do not yet distinguish the two implementations. Reflection alone also does not imply a resonance pole; a prompt constant reflection coefficient is a counterexample.

LVK's GWTC-3 analysis reports a damping-time deviation $\delta\hat\tau_{220}=0.14^{+0.11}_{-0.11}$ when multiplying posteriors and $0.13^{+0.21}_{-0.22}$ under hierarchical combination [29]. These are 90% credible intervals. The first excludes zero; the second includes it. The analysis discusses positive bias and possible prior effects, and does not claim a violation of GR. We do not convert the quoted interval width into a one-standard-deviation error.

A robust intrinsic departure from the predicted Kerr response, after the relevant astrophysical and waveform effects are accounted for, would challenge the adopted minimal implementation. It would not identify reflection as the cause or prove a particular quotient geometry. Echo-like propagation effects must likewise be distinguished from a new horizon reflection law.

Setting the seam weight to zero IS a choice within this action, and A.13 makes it explicitly, having withdrawn the two arguments that would have removed the choice; the structure a nonzero weight would carry is worked out separately anyway, because a seam action carrying a scale is not excluded and its reader is owed the shape a reflecting implementation would have. A.10 and the companion do that in three steps: what the kernel ratio of the companion's worked instance becomes at a general bifurcate Killing horizon, and how much of it survives without de Sitter's maximal symmetry; what a latitude-dependent horizon response does to the multipoles, which for a *scalar* field is an $\ell\to\ell\pm2$ mixing at fixed $m$ forced by the evenness of the transverse parity and switched on by spin alone, and which does not carry over to gravitational perturbations, whose spin-weighted harmonics admit $\ell\to\ell\pm1$ as the companion shows; and what the antilinear lift's action $\omega\to\omega^*$ requires of a reflecting horizon's spectrum, namely, for a constant reflectivity, that a complex one break the $\omega\to-\omega^*$ mirror symmetry while a real one cannot. None of the three supplies an amplitude, because the matching law above is what would fix it. They constrain the shape a reflecting implementation must have, and the minimal implementation adopted here sets all three to zero.

**A.7 Positivity, the Hadamard condition, and the image term.** §3.1's two inequalities, derived.
Let $\mathcal{A}$ be the antipodal map (*A* is reserved here for the amplitude), take the conformally coupled
massless scalar on dS$_4$, where the Bunch–Davies kernel is elementary, and consider the following real,
de Sitter-invariant two-amplitude ansatz on one static patch:

> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> W(x,y)&=\tfrac12 i\Delta(x,y)+A\,G_{\mathrm{sym}}(Z)+B\,G(-Z),\\
> G(Z)&=\frac{H^2}{8\pi^2(1-Z)},\qquad Z(x,\mathcal A y)=-Z(x,y).
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> The antipode pairs each cover mode with itself, antilinearly:
> $$
> y_n\circ\mathcal A=-\bar y_n,
> $$
> $$
> \langle a^\dagger a\rangle=\nu,\qquad
> \langle aa\rangle=\langle a^\dagger a^\dagger\rangle=\kappa,
> \qquad A=2\nu+1,\quad B=-2\kappa,
> $$
> $$
> \sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\nu+\tfrac12+\kappa&0\\0&\nu+\tfrac12-\kappa\end{pmatrix},
> \qquad
> \Omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},
> $$
> $$
> \sigma+\frac{i}{2}\Omega\succeq0
> \Longleftrightarrow\nu\ge0\ \text{and}\ \nu(\nu+1)\ge\kappa^2
> \Longleftrightarrow A\ge1\ \text{and}\ A^2-B^2\ge1.
> $$
> Since $G(-Z)=H^2/[8\pi^2(1+Z)]$ is regular as $Z\to1$, Hadamard normalisation gives
> $A=1$ and hence $B=0$.

Within the displayed de Sitter-invariant two-amplitude ansatz, the mode blocks may be chosen
diagonal in this basis and are described by the 2 × 2 covariance $\sigma$. Positivity,
$\sigma$ + (*i*/2)$\Omega$ $\ge$ 0, then says that the spectral weight of the symmetric part must dominate the
commutator $\tfrac12$*i*$\Delta$ mode by mode, which in the amplitudes is $A^2$ $-$ $B^2$ $\ge$ 1 (checked against direct
diagonalisation on a 301 × 601 grid in ($\nu$,$\kappa$): 180901/180901, no mismatches). The Hadamard step is
short-distance: the image term is regular as *Z* $\to$ 1; its only pole sits at *Z* = $-1,$ which a pair
of points reaches only on the horizon; so the whole coincidence singularity of *W* is *A* times the
cover's, and fixing it to the cover's geometric value *is* *A* = 1, whence *B* = 0. The tested projected image
prescription, (*A*,*B*) = (1,1) or ($\nu$,$\kappa$) = (0,$- \tfrac12$), misses positivity by exactly $\tfrac14$; it assigns zero
variance to one quadrature, $\sigma$_x = 0, minimum eigenvalue $-0.2071067812,$ and by Reeh–Schlieder the
offending test function can be taken supported inside one static patch, so the failure is already
local. The Mottola–Allen $\alpha$-vacua give the positive comparison family,
with *A* = (1+|*c*|$^2$)/(1$-$|*c*|$^2$) and *B* = 2|*c*|cos $\beta$/(1$-$|*c*|$^2$).
A nonzero image coefficient requires *A* > 1, contrary to the Hadamard normalisation *A* = 1.

**A.9 A selected time-symmetric response has zero net dissipative work.** §3.3's vanishing, derived. The antipode is an
isometry that reverses time orientation, so it exchanges the past and future light cones and
therefore $G_{\mathrm{ret}}$ $\circ$ (*A*×*A*) = $G_{\mathrm{adv}}$. The Wheeler–Feynman exchange [43]
is also checked numerically: max |$G_{\mathrm{ret}}$(*Ax*,*Ay*) $-$ $G_{\mathrm{adv}}$(*x*,*y*)| = 0.000e+00 for four masses;
hence **within the retarded/advanced affine family** the unique invariant combination is the even
one. This does not make the complete quotient Green problem unique without boundary data. For two
sources at separation *R* coupled through a stationary reciprocal mutual kernel *K*,

> $$
> W=\int dt\,\bigl[\dot q_2(t)\Phi_1(t)+\dot q_1(t)\Phi_2(t)\bigr],
> \qquad \Phi_i=\frac{K\ast q_i}{4\pi R}.
> $$
>
> For an even kernel, $K(t-t')=K(t'-t)$, and the integrand is
> $(\partial_t+\partial_{t'})[q_2(t)q_1(t')]K(t-t')$.
> Because $(\partial_t+\partial_{t'})K(t-t')=0$, that integrand is the total
> derivative $(\partial_t+\partial_{t'})\{q_2(t)q_1(t')K(t-t')\}$; with matching
> steady-state periods or vanishing all-history boundary terms it integrates to
> $W=0$. What vanishes is the symmetric derivative acting on the kernel, not the
> derivative acting on the product.
> In frequency space, $\widetilde G_{\mathrm{sym}}=\cos(\omega R)$ is real and
> $\max|\operatorname{Im}\widetilde G_{\mathrm{sym}}|=0$; meanwhile
> $\tfrac12\widetilde\Delta=i\sin(\omega R)$ carries the dissipative part.
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> W(G_{\mathrm{ret}})&\simeq-1.45221\times10^{-2},&
> W(G_{\mathrm{adv}})&\simeq+1.45221\times10^{-2},\\
> W(G_{\mathrm{sym}})&=0,&
> W(\tfrac12\Delta)&\simeq-1.45221\times10^{-2}=W(G_{\mathrm{ret}}).
> \end{aligned}
> $$

The nonzero controls are rounded finite-difference illustrations from, using a
central derivative step 2 $\times10^{-4}$; they are not continuum integrals accurate to ten decimal places.
Independent analytic-derivative R quadrature and a closed-form Gaussian-overlap calculation give
*W*($G_{\mathrm{ret}}$) = $-1.4522123062\times10^{-2}$ and *W*($G_{\mathrm{adv}}$) = $+1.4522123062\times10^{-2}$. The finite-difference
bias is about 1.03 $\times10^{-10}$ and decreases fourfold when that step is halved. The exact zero for the
selected symmetric kernel comes from the integration-by-parts argument, not a rounded output.

For a complete steady-state response that is this reciprocal even combination, the absorptive
operator vanishes and so does **total cycle-averaged secular work**. This does not set instantaneous
work, individual-source transfers or outgoing-wave amplitude to zero; nor does it include an
independent homogeneous or environmental response. The commutator $G_{\mathrm{ret}}$ $-$ $G_{\mathrm{adv}}$ identifies the
part the selected classical response omits, but symmetrizing one kernel does not prove the full field
theory classical. Weisberg and Huang report the observed-to-GR orbital-decay ratio for PSR B1913+16
as 0.9983 $\pm$ 0.0016 [21]; the zero-damping complete-response implementation is excluded by it.

**A.10 The law is the KMS condition off the surface, and how far the horizon ratio carries.** The
objection to meet first is that the reciprocal law is definitional: it holds for any involution and
any positive kernel, so on $B$ it constrains nothing. That objection is right, and it is right off
$B$ as well. With $R(x,y)=W(x,JP_\perp y)/W(x,Jy)$ and $P_\perp^2=\mathbb 1$, substitution alone
gives $R(x,P_\perp y)=R(x,y)^{-1}$ at every separation, so the identity carries no information
anywhere and no temperature enters it. What carries the argument off $B$ is not the identity but a
match, and the boost separation that has not yet dropped out is what leaves room for one. The
companion derives the image kernel $G_{\rm img}\propto\operatorname{sech}^2(Ht/2)$ from the geometry
of the antipodal map on the central worldline, with no temperature anywhere in that derivation; the
form is quoted here because what follows uses only the form. Requiring that geometric object to be a
thermal half-period shift $W(t-i\beta/2)$ of the Bunch-Davies kernel then fixes $\beta$. The match
holds to $4\times10^{-17}$ at $\beta=2\pi/H$ and fails by $0.115$ ten per cent away on either side,
fifteen orders worse, with a free minimisation returning $6.28320$ against $2\pi/H=6.28319$. But it
holds equally at every odd multiple $\beta=(2j+1)2\pi/H$, because
$\sinh(z-i(2j+1)\pi/2)=(-1)^{j}i\cosh z$ and squaring removes the sign, so the match alone
determines $\beta$ only modulo odd multiples. What picks the fundamental is primitivity, which is
part of the KMS condition rather than an addition to it: a function of period $2\pi/H$ has period
$3\times2\pi/H$ as well, and the KMS temperature is the primitive period. No match occurs below the
fundamental, which is what primitivity requires and is checked. The temperature is returned by that match
and is not inserted. Only then does $\alpha^2=1$ read $W(t-i\beta)=W(t)$, which is the KMS
condition; and we note plainly that this last step is a consistency check rather than a derivation,
since $\operatorname{csch}^2$ has period $2\pi i/H$ however it was obtained. In the frequency domain
the same statement is that $e^{\beta E/2}$ squares to the KMS factor $e^{\beta E}$: the fold's map
is the square root of the KMS transformation, and the horizon temperature is returned rather than
assumed.

None of that is new physics and it is not offered as any, since $\alpha$ contains the wedge
reflection and obtaining a horizon temperature from smoothness is standard. The point is defensive
and worth making anyway: the law's triviality on $B$ is a constraint degenerating rather than a
constraint missing, and the ninety-degree equality is the boundary value of something that fixes a
temperature. What none of it touches is the reduction to $W_B$ itself, which is the step this
appendix rests on and does not prove. The companion carries the rest of what the law reaches.

**How far the horizon ratio carries.** The companion obtains $G_\alpha/G_J\to\tan^2(\gamma/2)$ at the
de Sitter horizon for the conformally coupled scalar in the Bunch-Davies state. This section separates
the part of that result which belongs to horizons from the part which belongs to that spacetime and
that state, because the two have been conflated, including by us.

Let $B$ be the bifurcation surface of a bifurcate Killing horizon. The wedge reflection $J$ fixes $B$
pointwise; $P_\perp$ is a free involutive isometry of $B$ and moves it; $\alpha=J\circ P_\perp$. The
status of $P_\perp$ differs between the two settings and the difference matters. On de Sitter nothing
is chosen: $\alpha$ is the antipodal involution of the cover, $J$ is fixed by the wedge-reflection
theorems, and $P_\perp$ is whatever their composition makes it, the $(-1)^\ell$ parity of §2.1. At a
general horizon $B$ may admit several free involutive isometries and nothing here selects one, so
$P_\perp$ is an input to the statements below and not a consequence of them. What is computed is what
follows once it is named.
Taking both arguments to $B$, the boost separation drops out of both kernels and

$$
\frac{G_\alpha}{G_J}\longrightarrow\frac{W_B(x,P_\perp y)}{W_B(x,y)},
$$

the state's own correlator restricted to $B$, at the reflected separation over the direct one. This
uses no metric on $B$, no choice of state and nothing about the ambient spacetime beyond the
near-horizon form. It is why each kernel is singular exactly where its own map brings the arguments
together, and that much is general.

The reduction rests on three properties and on nothing else, which is worth setting out
because the rest of this appendix leans on it. Expand both kernels in transverse modes on
$B$. Since $P_\perp$ is an isometry of $B$ it acts on the transverse labels alone, and
since it commutes with the boost the boost factor $R_\ell(\Delta\tau)$ is common to the
two:

$$
G_J=\sum_\ell R_\ell(\Delta\tau)\,Y_\ell(x)Y_\ell(y)^*,\qquad
G_\alpha=\sum_\ell R_\ell(\Delta\tau)\,Y_\ell(x)Y_\ell(P_\perp y)^*.
$$

At $\Delta\tau=0$, which is both points on $B$, the common $R_\ell(0)$ is exactly what
defines the restriction $W_B$, so the ratio is $W_B(x,P_\perp y)/W_B(x,y)$ whatever
$R_\ell$ may be. Checked with three unrelated and strongly $\ell$-dependent boost factors
on a round $B$, where the antipodal insertion is $(-1)^\ell$: the reflected sum equals the
direct sum at the supplementary angle to $7\times10^{-17}$. No maximal symmetry, no round $B$, no particular
state and no closed form enters. Of the three properties the third carries the content,
namely that the $\Delta\tau\to0$ restriction exists at all; for a Hadamard state it does
away from transverse coincidence, which is where the ratio is evaluated, but a restriction
to a codimension-two surface is not automatic and we do not prove it. The first two are
geometry.

It is a limit and not an identity, and the same script shows the boost dependence does
not cancel away from $B$: at $\gamma=110^\circ$ the ratio moves by $1.2\times10^{-4}$ at
$\Delta\tau=0.05$ and by thirty per cent at $\Delta\tau=1.5$.

The reduction has a rate, which is worth having because any horizon calculation is done
at small but nonzero lapse. The boost separation enters both kernels only through the
coefficient $\sqrt{(1-r_1^2)(1-r_2^2)}=N_1N_2$, so it does not cancel *between* them; it
vanishes in each separately as $B$ is approached. The residual dependence is then first
order in $N^2$, with an angle-dependent constant measured at $rH=0.9999$ as $12.1$ at
$60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$, $61.8$ at $150^\circ$, $517$ at $170^\circ$, and exactly zero
at $90^\circ$. With $N^2\simeq d^2/16M^2$ for a
proper distance $d$ above a Schwarzschild horizon, the $B$-form is good to a per cent
within roughly $0.03$ of a Schwarzschild radius at moderate angles, and only much closer
than that near the antipodal one, where the fold kernel's own pole is being approached.
Two things follow. The reduction is a controlled limit with a calculable correction
rather than an asymptotic gesture. And the ninety-degree equality is the one angle at
which the $B$-form is exact at every radius, so it alone carries no truncation error,
which is a second reason to lean on it rather than on values elsewhere.

One more statement is general, and it was missed because the earlier work asked for a
value rather than a law. Write $R(x,y)=W_B(x,P_\perp y)/W_B(x,y)$. Replacing $y$ by
$P_\perp y$ and using $P_\perp^2=1$,

$$
R(x,y)\,R(x,P_\perp y)=1,
$$

identically. The proof is that displayed line and nothing else enters it, so the law
holds for every state, every correlator, every metric on $B$ and every free involution,
since it uses only that $P_\perp$ is one. On a round $B$ with an isotropic state
$P_\perp$ carries $\gamma$ to $\pi-\gamma$ and the law reads $R(\pi-\gamma)R(\gamma)=1$,
so $\log R$ is odd about $\gamma=\pi/2$ whenever $R>0$. The
law is exact by the displayed line, so the numerics that accompany it confirm an
implementation and not a mathematical fact; they are reported at
better than $2\times10^{-14}$ across ten positive decreasing profiles at four radii and four
hundred angles, and four thousand random configurations at unequal radii and nonzero
time separation, in that spirit. The oddness needs $\log R$ to be a function it makes sense to
expand: $R$ has a zero at one coincidence and a pole at the other, so $\log R$ diverges
at both ends, and it lies in $L^2(\sin\gamma\,d\gamma)$ exactly when those divergences
are logarithmic. Hadamard states give power-law singularities and therefore
$\log R\sim\log\gamma$, which is square-integrable; a state with an essential
singularity there would not be, and the multipole statement below would not apply to it.

Three consequences follow that the pointwise statement did not supply. The unit value at
$\gamma=\pi/2$ is the fixed point of the law rather than an independent fact, so it needs
no isotropy argument and no state. $\tan^2(\gamma/2)$ satisfies the law, which is why the
withdrawn universality looked right for as long as it did: what is universal is the
functional equation, and the conformal scalar's closed form is one solution of it. And
the law fixes a sign across the whole surface rather than at one angle. For any
correlator decreasing in separation, $R<1$ wherever $x$ lies nearer $y$ than $P_\perp y$
and $R>1$ where it does not, so the fold suppresses correlations on the near side of the
involution's equidistant locus and enhances them on the far side, with the crossing
exactly on that locus. Magnitude belongs to the state and the spacetime; the sign and the
locus do not.

How far the law reaches, what it measures when a state falls short of its own fold image, and what it says about the companion's closed-de Sitter calculation's branch weights are taken up separately in the companion, so that this appendix can return to the question it set: how much of the companion's value survives.

The value at a given angle is not. On a round $B$ with an isotropic state the ratio is
$G(\pi-\gamma)/G(\gamma)$, and three features of it hold for every such state: it vanishes like
$\gamma^2$, it equals exactly one at $\gamma=\pi/2$, and it has a $(\pi-\gamma)^{-2}$ pole. The first
and third are the Hadamard singularity at each coincidence; the second needs only that a point at
$\pi/2$ from $x$ has its antipode at $\pi/2$ as well. Nothing beyond those three is universal. Three
profiles with the same short-distance behaviour at both coincidences give 3.00, 4.00 and 2.75 at
$\gamma=2\pi/3$: the chordal form $1/(1-\cos\gamma)$, the arclength form $1/\gamma^2$, and a chordal
form carrying an asymmetric finite-separation factor (, log-slopes
$+2.000$ and $-2.000$ at the two ends for all three, and unity at $\pi/2$ to twelve digits).
$\tan^2(\gamma/2)$ is the first of them, exact on de Sitter because that state's two-point function is
exactly $1/(1-Z)$ at every separation, which is a consequence of maximal symmetry and not of the
horizon.

It is therefore not established for the Hartle-Hawking state on Schwarzschild. An earlier
near-horizon treatment of ours obtained it there by expanding about $r=2M$ to ${\rm Rindler}\times
S^2$ and using a flat transverse chord; the chord approximates the separation on a curved sphere
only at small angle, so that argument does not reach the angles at which it was evaluated, so it is
not a derivation. What survives on Schwarzschild is the general ratio above and the three universal
features, all of which $\tan^2(\gamma/2)$ happens to satisfy.

Kerr keeps the structure and loses the closed form. Its bifurcation surface carries

$$
ds^2=\rho_+^2\,d\theta^2+\frac{(r_+^2+a^2)^2\sin^2\theta}{\rho_+^2}\,d\phi^2,
\qquad \rho_+^2=r_+^2+a^2\cos^2\theta,
$$

which is axisymmetric and even in $\cos\theta$, so $P_\perp:(\theta,\phi)\mapsto(\pi-\theta,\phi+\pi)$
is an exact isometry, free and involutive, checked to $1.8\times10^{-15}$. The general ratio therefore
applies. But $B$ is squashed, with
$g_{\theta\theta}({\rm pole})/g_{\theta\theta}({\rm equator})=1+a^2/r_+^2$, equal to 1.024, 1.072,
1.167, 1.393 and 1.753 at $a/M=0.3$, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9 and 0.99 and to unity at $a=0$. Isotropy fails, and
with it any closed form and the unit value *at that angle*. The reciprocal law survives, because it needs
only the involution and Kerr keeps that, and it still holds to $6\times10^{-15}$ at
$a/M=0.3$, $0.7$ and $0.95$. What the law then locates is the set where
$W_B(x,P_\perp y)=W_B(x,y)$, and identifying that set with the curve equidistant from $y$
and $P_\perp y$ requires the state's restriction to $B$ to depend on those points only
through geodesic distance there. Given that, the locus is a deformed curve in place of a
great circle, and six unrelated distance-only profiles return $R=1$ to twelve digits at
the same point on it. That check confirms the
geometry and not the state. The state, though, turns out to cost less than we first
allowed. $P_\perp$ factors as a rotation by $\pi$ about the axis composed with the
equatorial reflection $\theta\mapsto\pi-\theta$, and Kerr has both symmetries, so an
axisymmetric equatorially symmetric restriction is $P_\perp$-invariant without that
having to be assumed; kernels built from the invariants such a state may depend on are
invariant to $10^{-15}$ over $2\times10^4$ random pairs. Then $W_B(x,P_\perp y)=W_B(P_\perp x,y)$ and the
locus where $R=1$ exists and is well defined: it is the set on which $P_\perp x$ and $x$
correlate equally with $y$.

What does fail is its identification with the *geodesic* equidistant curve, because an
invariant kernel on a squashed sphere is not a function of distance: two pairs whose
geodesic separations agree to $9\times10^{-10}$ return kernel values differing by $0.018$
in the same script. So spin moves the locus off the equidistant curve, and computing where
it goes needs the state and not only the metric. That is a smaller gap than the one we
recorded before, and it is the one that remains. The departure is switched on
by spin alone and vanishes identically for a non-rotating hole. The companion shows it is not a separate fact.

**A.13 Transparent matching is adopted, and the corner term is the only seam of its kind.** The
result first, and what it is not. One thing holds and it is narrow. Among quadratic boundary terms
at the specified codimension-one interface, the corner term is the one that can carry a
dimensionless coefficient and still act. That is not a classification of actions on $B$ itself,
which is codimension two, where $\int_B d^2x\,\phi^2$ already has a dimensionless coefficient for a
four-dimensional scalar; the concession below says so, and the opening may not claim more than the
concession allows. Within that action $r$ cannot depend on frequency, and unitarity fixes the
matching to $r(\kappa)=(1-\kappa^2)/(1+\kappa^2)$, $t(\kappa)=2\kappa/(1+\kappa^2)$.

What does *not* hold is a derivation of $\kappa=1$ from regularity. One route argues from finite relative entropy against the transparent case and
was circular. The second argued that a constant reflectivity puts an image term in the two-point
function which diverges between mirror-placed points at spacelike separation, and that no Hadamard
state is singular there. That argument is empty, for a reason worth stating precisely. The image structure
$W_0(x-y)+rW_0(x-\bar y)$ has same-side support. For two points on the same side the coincidence
$x=\bar y$ means $x+y=0$, which a sum of two negatives never reaches, so the singularity is
unreachable where the term exists. For points on opposite sides the coincidence *is* reachable, but
summing both incident channels of a unitary two-port defect cancels the image coefficient
identically, $rt+t(-r)=0$, so the term is absent where the singularity could be reached. Where it
exists it cannot bite; where it could bite it does not exist. This is the ordinary structure of a
point defect on a line, reflection terms having same-side support and transmission terms
opposite-side support.

So transparency is adopted here and not derived. The null Kerr implementation the rest of the
paper uses is unaffected, since it only ever needed a frequency-independent reflectivity and a
stated choice of it. Recovering a derivation would need: the covariance of the actual seam action, with both incident channels and its actual domain,
computed rather than modelled by a one-dimensional image ansatz.

The rest of this appendix is how that was reached and what it costs, because the corner term is
ultraviolet-incomplete and four earlier routes to the coefficient failed. Those failures are
statements about the seam rather than about the attempts. §3.4 records that three routes to fixing
the seam's one free coefficient are closed: symmetry and constraint closure admit the whole family,
the cover's vanishing $H^1$ leaves no period to quantize against, and the target's trivial $\pi_1$
supplies no field content. We attempted a fourth, an external measurement, and it fails for a reason
that is a statement about the seam rather than about the measurement.

Parametrise the seam by $\kappa$ in the corner term $(\kappa/2)q^TJ\dot q$. The reflection and
transmission that term produces are not quoted from anywhere; they follow from it. Varying
$S_c=(\kappa/2)\int dt\,(q_1\dot q_2-q_2\dot q_1)$ and integrating by parts in $t$ leaves
$\kappa(\dot q_2\,\delta q_1-\dot q_1\,\delta q_2)$, which against the bulk variation
$-\phi'\delta\phi$ gives the matching conditions $\phi_L'(0)=\pm\kappa\dot q_2$ and
$\phi_R'(0)=-\kappa\dot q_1$. With $\phi=e^{ikx}+re^{-ikx}$ on the left and $te^{ikx}$ on the
right at frequency $\omega=k$, these read $1-r=\kappa t$ and $t=\kappa(1+r)$, whose unique
solution is

$$
r(\kappa)=\frac{1-\kappa^2}{1+\kappa^2},\qquad
t(\kappa)=\frac{2\kappa}{1+\kappa^2},\qquad r^2+t^2=1.
$$

The other sign gives $r=(1+\kappa^2)/(1-\kappa^2)$, which is not a relabelling of the same
seam but a branch that fails unitarity: at $\kappa=\tfrac12$ it returns $r=5/3$ and $t=4/3$,
so $r^2+t^2=41/9$, and the violation grows with $\kappa$. Unitarity therefore selects the sign
rather than leaving it as a convention. A full two-channel scattering matrix with both bulk actions,
normals and incoming channels specified is not constructed here. Both
expressions and the unitarity are verified against the matching condition at
$\kappa=0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,1,2,4$ to machine precision. So $\kappa=1$ is the transparent point,
$r=0$ and $t=1$, and $\kappa=0$ and $\kappa\to\infty$ are the two perfect mirrors.

Because $\kappa$ is dimensionless and the corner term carries no other scale,
$r$ and $t$ cannot depend on frequency. That is the whole problem. A seam
reflecting with the same amplitude at every frequency, including arbitrarily
short wavelengths, is not a physical interface: real interfaces become
transparent in the ultraviolet. The corner term as written therefore needs a
scale it does not have.

The symptom is visible in any quantity built from it. Summing the two scattering
channels at two ports of separation $2a$ in the one-dimensional model of gives a ratio of image to local kernel
of the form $t(C+r)/(1+rC)$, where

$$
C(a)=\frac{\int dk\,\cos 2ka/2\omega_k}{\int dk\,1/2\omega_k}.
$$

The numerator converges and the denominator diverges logarithmically, so $C$ is
not a property of the port geometry at all: it falls steadily as the cutoff is
lifted, running $0.958$, $0.603$, $0.497$, $0.441$, $0.371$, $0.315$ at $a=0.02$
for $k_{\max}$ from $20$ to $2\times10^4$. In the limit the cutoff is removed
$C\to0$ and the ratio tends to $tr$, which vanishes at $\kappa=1$ where the companion's
established central-worldline result has magnitude one. The local kernel in that
model is itself logarithmically divergent with no seam at all, while the companion's
$A_0=1/12\pi^2$ is finite, which shows the one-dimensional model was never a
model of the companion's object: a force-noise kernel is not a field two-point function.

No measurement protocol is available in which two even/odd measurements at different port
separations were said to fix $\kappa$. They cannot, because the quantity being varied was set by the
regulator rather than by the separation.

What the exercise establishes is a condition on the seam action rather than a
value for its coefficient, and the right form of that condition took two attempts.

The wrong form first, because it was ours and it is instructive. Any seam whose
*relative entropy against the transparent case* is to be finite needs $r(k)\to0$ as
$k\to\infty$; $r$ is frequency-independent here; so $r=0$ and $\kappa=1$. That
argument is circular. Finiteness is being asked of a comparison whose reference is
the very seam the argument selects, and since two constant reflectivities differ at
arbitrarily short distance, *every* pair in the family has infinite relative entropy
against every other. "Finite relative entropy against $X$" therefore selects $X$ for
any $X$ in this family, and selects nothing. We record it because the conclusion
below is the same and the reasoning is not, and a reader is owed the difference.

The non-circular form asks the question of one state instead of two. With a constant
reflectivity the seam's two-point function carries an image term,

$$
W(x,y)=W_0(x-y)+r\,W_0(x-\bar y),
$$

with $\bar y$ the reflection of $y$. Both sides of the seam are physical here, so
$x=\bar y$ is reached by two *distinct* points, mirror-placed across the seam and
spacelike separated from one another. The image term is singular exactly there.
A two-point function singular at spacelike separation violates the microlocal
spectrum condition, so the state is not Hadamard, and that is a statement about the
state alone with no reference and nothing to compare against. Smearing the image
term against $r(\omega)$ makes the distinction explicit: $\int d\omega\,r(\omega)\cos\omega\epsilon$
grows without bound as $\epsilon\to0$ for constant $r$ and converges for any
$r(\omega)\to0$, checked for an exponential and a Lorentzian fall-off. Hadamard regularity is the condition
$r(\omega)\to0$, and within the corner term, where $r$ cannot depend on frequency,
it holds at one point of the family: $\kappa=1$, $t=1$, the transparent seam.

The step that carries the weight is the one a referee will press: why are the mirror pairs spacelike
separated rather than null or timelike, and why is the image term a divergence rather than a finite
correction. They are always spacelike, and that is the defining property of the pair of wedges and
not a feature of a chosen slice. Writing the wedges as $z>|t|$ and $z<-|t|$, one point in each gives
$z_1-z_2>|t_1|+|t_2|\ge|t_1-t_2|$, so $(\Delta z)^2>(\Delta t)^2$ and the interval is positive;
$4\times10^5$ sampled pairs return none that is not spacelike, the smallest interval among them
being $9.2\times10^{-3}$. The image term's singular locus $x=Jy$ lies inside that set, so the
divergence occupies a codimension-zero family of configurations rather than one contrived point. And
it is not a finite correction: approaching the locus, the image term exceeds the direct one by a
factor that grows without bound, reaching $2.5\times10^{8}$ at a separation of $10^{-4}$, so it
cannot be absorbed into a redefinition. A second
criterion lands on the same point by a different route, and it is worth having because the first is
a regularity argument and this one is variational. A free-fermion chain with a defect bond is the
same two-port scattering problem, with transmission $T=4t_d^2/(1+t_d^2)^2$, in a setting where
entanglement is computable exactly. The total entropy across the defect is *not* a function of $T$:
a strong bond builds a dimer across the cut whose local contribution swamps the
transmission-dependent part, and $t_d=0.5$ and $t_d=2$ give entropies differing by more than a
factor of two at identical $T$. The coefficient of $\log L$ is a function of $T$ and of nothing
else, which is worth establishing rather than assuming: a bond defect and a site defect tuned to the
same transmission return the same coefficient to within $0.6$ per cent at $T=0.95$, $0.88$, $0.64$
and $0.30$, although they perturb the chain differently; the $T=0.88$ pair is $0.89945$ against
$0.90465$, a $0.58$ per cent difference. These are finite-size fits and not a proof of universality. That coefficient is maximal at $T=1$,
with both neighbours below it. Its
symmetry under $t_d\to1/t_d$ is inherited from the symmetry of $T$ under that map rather than being
an independent structure, so it is the seam's rapidity inversion seen again and not a second fact.
The transparent seam is therefore the one that maximises the entanglement between the two sides,
which for a framework claiming the sheets are joined is the natural way to say how strongly.

The selection is soft and should not be oversold: a five per cent reflection retains
$99.7$ per cent of the coefficient, so this identifies the maximum without excluding its
neighbourhood. The exclusion is the regularity argument's work, not this one's. And the
lattice shares the scattering problem rather than the dynamics; the entropy computed is
the chain's.

The argument reaches further than the constant case, which is worth showing because the
obvious objection is that it does not. A seam might be non-linear, with a reflectivity
that vanishes at small field amplitude and switches on above some $A_0$; such a seam would
be transparent wherever the field is weak, and one might expect the short-distance
condition to miss it. It does not, and the reason is that short distance is not small
amplitude. Vacuum fluctuations diverge as the separation closes, the amplitude a probe at
separation $s$ samples running as $1/2\pi s$, so for any finite $A_0$ the seam is switched
on below $s_*=1/2\pi A_0$ and its reflectivity tends to one as the separation shrinks
further: $0.99$ at $s_*/10$ and $0.9999$ at $s_*/100$, independently of $A_0$. The image term therefore arrives at full
strength precisely where the argument looks, and the conclusion is stronger for a
non-linear seam than for a constant one rather than weaker.

The scale dependence is real and runs opposite to the intuition that suggests it. A seam reflecting
at large amplitude is transparent in the infrared and reflecting in the ultraviolet, not transparent
where we can look and active near a singularity. That is the expected sign for a boundary coupling
carrying a dimensionful scale, which is irrelevant in the infrared and grows toward the ultraviolet,
and it is why an ultraviolet condition catches it. It is also why no such seam shows in a waveform.
Ringdown sits at $0.374\,c^3/2\pi GM$, which is $41$ orders of magnitude below a Planck seam scale,
$37$ below a grand-unified one, $30$ below the $492$ PeV mass of the paper's §3.1 and $24$ below a TeV. That gap does not show that a ringdown cannot see
whatever a seam does at short distance; the inference runs the wrong way. Write the response as
$r(\omega/\Lambda)$ for a microscopic scale $\Lambda$. Astrophysical ringdown sits at
$\omega/\Lambda_P\simeq4\times10^{-40}$ for $10M_\odot$ and $4\times10^{-48}$ for $10^9M_\odot$,
which is the deep *infrared* of that function and not its ultraviolet. A condition forcing $r\to0$
at large argument constrains nothing there: $r(x)=e^{-x}$ obeys it and equals one to forty
significant figures at these arguments. A microscopic scale therefore does not make a seam invisible
at ringdown, and no statement about astrophysical waveforms follows from an ultraviolet condition
alone. The implementation adopted here is transparent at every frequency by choice, which is a
different matter and is unaffected.

What must still
be granted is that the seam state has to be Hadamard at all.What must still
be granted is that the seam state has to be Hadamard at all. That is the standard
admissibility condition, required for the stress tensor to renormalise, but it is an
assumption here; a reader who declines it recovers a free $\kappa$, and everything else
above is geometry.

It is not the case that no choice of $\kappa$ satisfies the ultraviolet condition. That is false:
$\kappa=1$ satisfies it, trivially and uniquely, and $10^5$ members sampled between $\kappa=e^{-5}$
and $e^{5}$ return exactly one with $|r|<10^{-14}$.

*The corner term is not one choice among many.* The three steps need a constant reflectivity, which
the action supplies by carrying no scale of its own, so the question is which boundary terms on $B$
can carry a dimensionless coefficient at all. At quadratic order there is one. A scalar has
dimension $(d-2)/2$ and a boundary density must have dimension $d-1$, so at $d=4$ only terms with
exactly one derivative qualify, namely $q\,\dot q$ and $q\,\nabla q$. The second is $\nabla(q^2)/2$
and integrates to zero on a closed bifurcation surface. The symmetric part of the first is
$\tfrac12\,d(q^2)/dt$ and drops out of the matching condition, which we check directly; the
antisymmetric pairing does not and is the corner term. So on a codimension-one matching surface "a seam
carrying no scale of its own" and "the corner term" name the same object at this order. On $B$
itself, which is codimension two, the count runs the other way: $g\int_B d^2x\,\phi^2$ has a
dimensionless $g$, and the prior objection to the counting above is correct and is not answered
here. Computing the reflectivity of a one-dimensional delta potential does not answer it: a
codimension-two defect does not scatter like a delta in one normal coordinate, needs its own
regularisation, and can generate logarithmic scales of its own. The rescue is withdrawn. Relatedly,
$r(\omega)\to0$ is not by itself Hadamard regularity, since removing a pointwise divergence does not
remove a wavefront singularity, and a proper microlocal treatment is not attempted here.

So the statement worth making is narrow and is stated narrowly: the dimensional counting above
concerns a specified codimension-one interface and does not classify every action that could
live on the bifurcation surface. What the three steps of this appendix need, and use, is a
reflectivity that does not depend on frequency; what they do not supply is a classification of
seams.

So the seam weight is not a free parameter of *this* action. Ordinary absorbing Kerr
dynamics is what the corner term gives rather than what was chosen for it. Three
limits. The three steps use a constant reflectivity, the image term it
puts in the two-point function, and the spacelike separation of mirror points; none of them
mentions the action. The action enters only by making $r$ frequency-independent, since
$\kappa$ is dimensionless and the corner term carries no other scale. So the argument reaches
every scale-free seam and not only the one written here, and what it says nothing about is a
seam carrying a scale of its own, which §3.4 already separates out. Hadamard regularity is the standard
admissibility condition for a state in curved spacetime and is assumed here rather
than derived. And the image-term structure is generic to a reflecting interface,
which is why it survives A.13's own warning that the one-dimensional model is not a
model of the companion's object: what is used is the scattering parametrisation and the
existence of an image term, not the model's kernel.

**A symmetry route, and why it fails.** It is natural to ask whether the fold's own
identification of the sheets fixes $\kappa$ without any ultraviolet input. Writing
$\kappa=e^{u}$, the family's transfer matrix is
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\cosh u&\sinh u\\\sinh u&\cosh u\end{smallmatrix}\right)$
to $1.8\times10^{-15}$, so seams in series add rapidities, $\kappa\to1/\kappa$ is the
group inverse, and reversing the crossing sends $u\to-u$. Requiring the seam to look
the same from either sheet would then give $u=0$ uniquely. It does not, and the
reason is computable rather than a matter of taste. The two channels are the sheets,
so the exchange is $E=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
which *anticommutes* with the corner term's complex structure
$J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Under the exchange
together with the time reversal the fold's map carries, $q\to Eq$ and
$\dot q\to-E\dot q$, so $(\kappa/2)q^TJ\dot q$ returns to itself for every $\kappa$,
verified on $2000$ random $(q,\dot q,\kappa)$. The fold's sheet symmetry is therefore
satisfied by the whole family and selects nothing. The route is closed, not open, and
the closure is what makes the Hadamard argument load-bearing rather than
confirmatory.

**If the seam carries the horizon's own scale.** The one escape from the above is the
one the argument itself points at: $r$ cannot depend on frequency *because the corner
term supplies no scale*, and a horizon supplies several. A seam matched at the
horizon may carry $T_H$, and then $r=r(\omega/T_H)$ with $r(\infty)=0$ satisfies
Hadamard regularity without being transparent. That is a different action owing its
own matching law, but it is not free, because two predictions follow before any
amplitude is chosen. Both $M\omega$ and $MT_H$ are dimensionless functions of $a/M$
alone, so $\omega/T_H$ depends on spin and not on mass: such a seam reflects
identically for a $10\,M_\odot$ and a $10^{9}\,M_\odot$ hole at the same spin, which
is a null test across eight orders of magnitude available from ringdowns in matching
spin bins. And with

$$
\frac{\omega}{T_H}=4\pi\,(M\omega)\,\frac{1+\sqrt{1-a^2}}{\sqrt{1-a^2}},
$$

equal to $8\pi M\omega$ at $a=0$ and diverging at extremality, spinning the hole up
pushes the ringdown to larger argument, where $r$ is smaller. Horizon reflectivity
effects are therefore largest at low spin and vanish as extremality is approached *for a response
depending on $\omega/T_H$ alone*, which §3.4's corrected co-rotating case is not: there the
amplitude rises from $9.8\times10^{-3}$ at zero spin to $0.444$ at $a=0.9$. An asymptotic falloff
does not imply global monotonicity, and a rotating response can depend on
$(\omega-m\Omega_H)/T_H$. What follows is therefore a property of the assumed response law,
for every fall-off; three unrelated ones give the same monotone ordering. Echo models with a reflecting wall at fixed
proper distance predict the opposite trend, since the light-crossing delay grows as
the horizon is approached. The two are separated by the sign of the trend with spin,
with no amplitude required from either. The quasinormal frequencies themselves are
not computed here; the ratio above is carried by $T_H$, which varies by a factor
$8.4$ between $a=0$ and $a=0.998$ while $M\omega$ varies by a factor of a few, so the
trend does not depend on values we have not computed.

The two predictions are not equally robust and the difference matters. Mass
independence survives any choice of borrowed scale, because every scale a horizon
can lend, the temperature, the horizon radius, the square root of the area, the
angular velocity, the curvature, is the mass times a function of spin, so the mass
cancels identically whichever is taken. The vanishing toward extremality does not:
it belongs to the temperature, which diverges at extremality and changes by a factor of
twelve across the spins sampled, where the radius and the area change by less than two and give
a nearly flat trend instead. An earlier note of ours attached
the extremality behaviour to any horizon scale, which is wrong. So the spin
dependence discriminates among reflecting implementations built from horizon scales,
while the mass independence is common to all of them.

We do not establish that the scale must be the horizon's, and it is worth saying plainly where that
leaves the claim, because the obvious reply is one word long: Planck. A seam could carry a scale of
external origin, and such a seam is neither transparent nor built from the horizon. It does not
escape the statement, but it changes it from a prohibition into a classification. An external scale
far above the horizon's puts the ringdown at $\omega/\Lambda_P$ between $4\times10^{-40}$ and
$4\times10^{-48}$ across astrophysical masses. The reciprocal quantity $M\Lambda_P$ runs from
$10^{38}$ to $10^{47}$ and is not the argument of the response. These arguments are the deep
infrared, an ultraviolet falloff condition does not constrain a response there, and the invisibility
claim is withdrawn. An external scale *comparable* to the
horizon's does show up, as a dependence on mass at fixed spin, and it would then be measuring that
scale rather than refuting anything; for that to be visible the scale must sit between about
$10^{-11}$ and $10^{-20}$ eV, where nothing in particle physics currently sits.

So the honest statement is a sorting rather than a no-go. Reflectivity mapped across
mass and spin separates three cases: none at any mass or spin, which is a scale-free
seam or any scale far above the horizon's and does not distinguish between them;
variation with spin alone, which is a horizon scale and is the fold's own branch, with
the trend fixed by which scale; and variation with mass at fixed spin, which is a scale
of external origin near the horizon's. That is a measurement programme, it costs no
amplitude, and unlike the dichotomy it does not rest on a lemma we cannot prove.

**A.17 How far the reciprocal law reaches.** A.10 establishes the law and its immediate consequences. This appendix collects what it reaches beyond the bifurcation surface: its algebraic form, the measure it supplies for a state falling short of its own fold image, and what that measure says about the branch weights of the closed-de Sitter overlap below.

The law does not need a manifold, and saying so settles how much of this belongs to
horizons and how much to the fold. For a state $\omega$ on an algebra carrying an
involutive automorphism $\sigma$, write $R(A,B)=\omega(A\sigma(B))/\omega(AB)$; the same
substitution gives $R(A,\sigma B)=1/R(A,B)$, needing only $\sigma^2={\rm id}$. Checked on
a random density matrix with a random involutive automorphism in eight dimensions over
four thousand operator pairs, to $2\times10^{-12}$. The geometric statement above is one instance.

Once the law is algebraic, the natural question is how far a state falls short of being
its own fold image, and the measure is the relative entropy $S(\omega\,\|\,\omega\circ\sigma)$.
Two properties of it belong to the involution rather than to relative entropy generally.
It is symmetric: relative entropy is invariant under a common automorphism, so
$S(\omega\circ\sigma\,\|\,\omega)=S(\omega\circ\sigma\,\|\,(\omega\circ\sigma)\circ\sigma)=S(\omega\,\|\,\omega\circ\sigma)$
once $\sigma^2={\rm id}$, and four hundred random state-and-involution pairs return an
asymmetry below $7\times10^{-15}$ against values of order unity. And it is second order in
the misalignment: mixing a fold-symmetric state toward a generic one, the divergence grows
as the square of $\|\omega-\omega\circ\sigma\|$ with a settled coefficient, vanishing only
at exact fold symmetry. Fold-symmetric states are
therefore a stationary point of this divergence and not merely its zero set.

The second-order behaviour is the part with consequences, because it fixes how
misalignment accumulates. Independent departures of size $\epsilon$ over $N$ events sum to
order $N\epsilon^2$ rather than $(N\epsilon)^2$, so the accumulation is diffusive and not
coherent, and a universe-wide tally is far smaller than a naive addition of amplitudes
suggests. We do not compute $\epsilon$ for any process and nothing in the fold supplies
it, so this is a scaling and not a number; turning it into one means computing the
misalignment a definite process produces, for which the branch decoherence of the closed-de Sitter overlap below is the
natural first case and is not attempted here.

Applying it to a definite process closes that gap. the closed-de Sitter overlap below's two branches carry environment
states of overlap $s$; writing the branch-label state as
$a\lvert+\rangle\lvert E_+\rangle+b\lvert-\rangle\lvert E_-\rangle$ and tracing out the
environment gives a two-by-two density matrix with diagonal $(\lvert a\rvert^2,\lvert
b\rvert^2)$ and off-diagonal carrying $s$. Because $\Theta$ is antilinear *and* exchanges
the branches, $\Theta\rho\Theta$ is $\rho$ with the two weights swapped. The misalignment
therefore turns on the branch weight and not on the coherence, and vanishes identically at
equal weight, at every overlap.

Two consequences. Decoherence regulates the divergence rather than causing it: at unequal
weight a coherent superposition is pure, and a pure state has infinite relative entropy
against anything off its support, while the fully decohered value is finite and closed
form, $S=(2p-1)\log[p/(1-p)]$, which is $8(p-\tfrac12)^2$ to leading order and reproduces
the coefficient the general argument gave. And the timing is early: using the closed-de Sitter overlap below's own
overlap with $aH=\cosh Ht$, which returns its quoted $Ht=1.28983$ at $aH=1.95374$, the
misalignment has settled onto its decohered value by the threshold.

That closes a possibility rather than opening one. A picture in which many small
unobserved events accumulate a misalignment across cosmic history does not apply here:
the measure turns on the branch weight alone, the weight stops evolving once the branches
decohere, and the fold sets it to one half by CPT. We compute no departure from one half
and nothing in the paper supplies one, so what the measure describes is a property of the
initial weights, frozen inside the first e-fold, and not a quantity that grows.

That last claim was too quick, and checking it reversed it. Equal weight at the fold point
does not propagate. $\Theta$ is antiunitary, and CPT covariance gives
$\Theta U\Theta^{-1}=U^{-1}$, which is not $[\Theta,U]=0$, so a state that is
$\Theta$-invariant at the fold point is not $\Theta$-invariant afterwards:
$\Theta\lvert\psi(t)\rangle=\lvert\psi(-t)\rangle$. The branch imbalance $\langle N\rangle$
is therefore an odd function of time, zero at the fold point and nonzero away from it,
checked to $2\times10^{-15}$ on a Hamiltonian built to satisfy $\Theta H\Theta^{-1}=H$.

What the oddness means is more useful than what it costs. $\langle N\rangle$ is neither
conserved nor zero on either sheet; what vanishes exactly is the sum over the pair, since
the mirror sheet's value at its time $t$ is ours at $-t$. The two sheets carry equal and
opposite branch imbalance at every time and the pair carries none. That is a joining of a
kind not otherwise in this paper: not a force between the sheets and not a shared boundary
condition, but a conserved quantity that exists only when both are counted.

It also corrects how the divergence should be read. $S(\omega\,\|\,\omega\circ\sigma)$ is
not a defect accumulating with cosmic time; the two sheets are meant to differ. What is
constrained is the antisymmetry, and that is exact. No rate is delivered: the growth
depends on the Hamiltonian, and the one used here is a random CPT-covariant matrix rather
than the closed-de Sitter overlap below's, so the shape is established and the number is not.

That last point needs a qualification, and it is the one that applies to the closed-de Sitter overlap below. The
calculation assumed a state evolving under a CPT-covariant Hamiltonian. Wheeler-DeWitt
supplies a constraint solution rather than an evolving state, and the no-boundary
wavefunction is real, $\psi\sim\cos S=\tfrac12(e^{iS}+e^{-iS})$, so the two branch
amplitudes are complex conjugates, their moduli are equal identically at every $aH$, and
there is no time dependence to track. In the closed-de Sitter overlap below's setting the imbalance vanishes for that
reason and not because of CPT.

The two conditions differ and the stronger one is imported. CPT covariance gives an
imbalance odd in time and generally nonzero; reality of the wavefunction gives zero
identically. Reality is not, however, only a choice of cosmological boundary
condition: the paragraphs below obtain it from $\Theta$-invariance instead, and §2.5 states
the commitment that makes that available. What a reader preferring a tunnelling
wavefunction loses is therefore the no-boundary route to reality, not reality itself,
provided they keep $\Theta$-invariance of physical states. What survives either way is the cross-sheet statement, since
$\langle N\rangle(t)+\langle N\rangle(-t)=0$ holds trivially when both vanish. The
conservation law is therefore the more robust of the two claims, and the equal weight on
a single sheet is the weaker.

That weaker claim can be improved, though not to a theorem. Reality of the wavefunction
need not be borrowed from the no-boundary proposal, because $\Theta$ is antilinear and
imposing $\Theta\lvert\psi\rangle=\lvert\psi\rangle$ *is* a reality condition. Writing
$\Theta=K\circ S$ with $K$ complex conjugation and $S$ linear, $\Theta^2=1$ requires
$S\bar S=1$, and in the branch basis with $S$ the branch swap the condition reads
$\psi_-=\bar\psi_+$, which is the no-boundary form and forces
$\lvert\psi_-\rvert=\lvert\psi_+\rvert$. Equal branch weight and $\Theta$-invariance are
the same condition, not two. The restriction is well defined: the Wheeler-DeWitt operator
is real, so its solution space is closed under conjugation, checked directly, and the
invariant part carries exactly half the real dimension, measured as the rank of
$(1+\Theta)/2$ applied to a complex basis at $d=1,2,3,5$. That is the same halving A.15 obtains at the
singularity through $(1+JU)/2$: two loci, one mechanism, an involution keeping its even
sector.

What this changes is the provenance of the assumption rather than its status. It is no
longer borrowed from Hartle and Hawking but stated in the fold's own terms. Two things it
does not do. It still needs the physical state to be a constraint solution rather than an
evolving one, since $\Theta$-invariance is not preserved under evolution, and timelessness
is a feature of the formulation rather than a theorem. And §2.4 gauges the *linear*
involution $P_\perp$, following Harlow and Numasawa; gauging an antilinear one is a
different operation which we do not perform. So $\Theta$-invariance is a condition the
fold may adopt naturally, not one it already carries.

That reach matters for the horizons A.10 cannot otherwise touch, and it also marks where
a natural repair fails. One might hope to move from $B$ to a late-time
cross-section, since the reduction needed only $\Delta\tau=0$ and a cross-section supplies
that by construction. It does not work: $J$ fixes $B$ pointwise and fixes nothing else on
the horizon, carrying every cross-section of $H^+$ to a cross-section of the other sheet's
$H^-$, so the two arguments no longer share a surface and the ratio is a correlator
between two horizon branches rather than one restricted to a single surface. The
reciprocal law itself is untouched by that, since both kernels send their second argument
to the same place and differ only by the transverse reflection. The obstruction was never
the absence of $B$; it is the absence of the second sheet. So the algebraic law transfers
to a collapse hole, whose settled exterior is stationary and axisymmetric and whose
transverse antipodal map is an automorphism of the exterior algebra, while
$\alpha=J\circ P_\perp$ as the second Keldysh leg does not, because no algebra supplies a
second sheet.

The obvious objection is that the law is definitional: it holds for any involution and
any positive kernel, so it constrains nothing. That is correct on $B$ and, as A.10 now records,
off it too, the identity being algebraic at every separation;
and the two are one equation in different limits. Off $B$ the boost separation has not
dropped out, and A.6's identification makes $\alpha$ act as the half-period continuation
$W(t-i\beta/2)$, which reproduces the image kernel there to $4\times10^{-17}$. Then
$\alpha^2=1$ reads $W(t-i\beta)=W(t)$, and that is not free: sweeping the shift period,
the condition fails by $O(25)$ at every value tried and holds only at $\beta=2\pi/H$ and
its multiples, while minimising the residual returns $6.2831853072$ against
$2\pi/H=6.2831853072$ from three of four sample times, the fourth a boundary artefact of
the search window carrying a residual of $45$. In
the frequency domain the same statement is that $e^{\beta E/2}$ squares to the full KMS
factor $e^{\beta E}$, so the fold's map is the square root of the KMS transformation.

None of that is new physics and it is not offered as any. $\alpha$ contains the wedge
reflection, which is the boost through imaginary angle $\pi$, so $\alpha^2=1$ says a
$2\pi$ Euclidean rotation is the identity; obtaining the horizon temperature from
smoothness is standard. The point is defensive and worth making anyway: the fold's
$\mathbb Z_2$ *is* that condition, so the reciprocal law's triviality on $B$ is the
degeneration of a constraint rather than the absence of one, and the equality at ninety
degrees is the boundary value of something that fixes a temperature. What none of this
touches is the reduction to $W_B$ itself, which is the step A.10 rests on and which is not
proved there.

**A.11 A latitude-dependent horizon response mixes $\ell$ by two.** The squashing of A.10 is one
function, and it has a second consequence that is easier to look for. Write the local anisotropy of
the bifurcation surface, azimuthal proper length per unit coordinate over meridional, as

$$
A(\theta)=\frac{\sqrt{g_{\phi\phi}}}{\sin\theta\,\sqrt{g_{\theta\theta}}}
=\frac{r_+^2+a^2}{r_+^2+a^2\cos^2\theta},
$$

verified against the induced metric to $2\times10^{-16}$ at four spins. It is unity at every latitude only when $a=0$, and
$A(\pi/2)/A(0)$ is exactly the squashing ratio $1+a^2/r_+^2$ of A.10, at every spin tested. The two
statements are one function seen twice.

Teukolsky's separation puts the horizon condition on the radial function alone, which works because
that condition is the same at every latitude. A response weighted by $A$ is not, so it cannot be
imposed mode by mode: it couples different $\ell$ at fixed $m$. Because $A$ is even in $\cos\theta$, its Legendre
expansion carries even terms only, so on a *scalar* field the coupling is $\ell\to\ell\pm2$,
$\ell\pm4$ and never $\ell\pm1$. Computed on a 200-node Gauss-Legendre rule, every odd
coefficient vanishes and the largest odd-parity entry of $\langle P_\ell|A|P_{\ell'}\rangle$ is
$2.7\times10^{-20}$.

That rule does not survive the move to gravitational perturbations. Teukolsky modes are
spin-weighted, $s=-2$, and spin-weighted harmonics do not carry the parity the scalar argument uses.
The decisive matrix element is non-zero:
$\langle{}_{-2}Y_{22}|\cos^2\theta|{}_{-2}Y_{32}\rangle=\sqrt{35}/21\simeq0.2817$, reproduced
numerically to $6\times10^{-16}$, against $2.7\times10^{-17}$ for the same element built from
Legendre functions. An even response can
therefore mix $\ell=2$ with $\ell=3$ at fixed $m=2$. The scalar coefficients below are correct; what
fails is carrying their selection rule into the gravitational case. The strength is $c_2/c_0$, which
is $-0.0017$, $-0.016$, $-0.046$, $-0.219$ and $-0.367$ at $a/M=0.1$, 0.3, 0.5, 0.9 and 0.99, and
identically zero at $a=0$.

The selection rule is the useful part, and it is worth being exact about what it can and cannot do.
It supplies no amplitude: the law that would fix how strongly a horizon response is weighted by $A$
is the matching law for a general rotating-horizon seam, which §3.4 still records as unconstructed. For the corner term the weight is adopted as zero rather than derived, A.13 having withdrawn both
arguments that would have derived it.
So the rule is not a falsifier of this paper. Read as a falsifier it is empty, because the
implementation defended here predicts no such response to begin with.

It is not a discriminator on somebody's positive detection either, and that is the claim being
withdrawn. The multipole structure of a ringdown anomaly does not decide whether the fold could be
its cause: a parity-even transverse map can produce $\ell\pm1$ mixing for spin-weighted modes, by
the element above, so an $\ell\pm1$ admixture rules nothing out and its absence confirms nothing.
What remains is the scalar statement and the general one: a response that depends on latitude
couples different $\ell$ at fixed $m$, and the size of that coupling still has no law to fix it.

A selection rule without a detectability budget is not yet a prediction, so here
is one, and the useful feature is that it is asymmetric. Take the seam weight $w$
to be bounded by the echo searches, since a reflecting seam is what those searches
look for, and write the fractional power going to $\ell=4$ as $w^2|c_2/c_0|$. A
three-sigma detection of that contamination then needs a fundamental-mode ringdown
signal-to-noise of roughly $3/(w\sqrt{|c_2/c_0|})$, which at $a=0.9$ and $w=10^{-2}$
is about 600 and at $w=10^{-3}$ about 6000. Current instruments reach a few tens on
loud events, so confirmation waits on next-generation detectors and, at the lower
weight, on nothing yet planned. These are order-of-magnitude figures and the
detector numbers are nominal.

No asymmetry survives the selection rule. The fold does not forbid $\ell\pm1$ identically for
gravitational modes, so there is no free exclusion and a target amplitude does have to be reached,
on both sides. The measurement side is not free in the same way, and we do not have a matching
formula for it. Telling a real odd-parity admixture from noise, from waveform systematics, or from
an $\ell=2$ overtone is itself amplitude-dependent, and the smaller nature's true leakage the more
signal-to-noise it takes to resolve, exactly as on the confirming side. So the direction of the
asymmetry is established and its size is not: quoting a figure of order tens for the refuting side
would be borrowing generic loud-event capability rather than computing anything, and we do not quote
one. A claim that is easier to refute than to confirm is the right way round, and it is the sense in
which this rule earns its place despite supplying no amplitude.

**A.12 The antilinear lift acts on the quasinormal spectrum as $\omega\to\omega^*$.** This follows
from the definitions in §2.1 with no further input. Acting on a mode $e^{-i\omega t}$, the pullback by
$\alpha$ reverses $t$, giving $e^{+i\omega t}$, and antilinearity then conjugates the coefficient,
giving $e^{-i\omega^*t}$. A mode damped in our time therefore pairs with one growing in our time,
which is not a pathology but the content of the CPT relation: the partner sheet carries the opposite
time orientation, so each sheet sees ordinary damping on its own clock, and the growth exists only
when one sheet is described in the other's.

That action has a consequence for a reflecting horizon, and we state it before computing it. The
Schwarzschild spectrum has the mirror symmetry $\omega\to-\omega^*$: if $\omega$ is a quasinormal
frequency then so is $-\omega^*$. The fold sends $\omega\to\omega^*$, so composing the two gives
$\omega\to-\omega$, and whether the fold's action is compatible with the spectrum's own symmetry is
therefore a question about the boundary condition. It has a two-line answer. Leaver's coefficients
are real in $\rho=-i\omega$, so the continued fraction obeys $\overline{F(\omega)}=F(-\omega^*)$ and
likewise for $G$, which we check at six frequencies, real and complex, with zero defect. Conjugating
the quasinormal condition $F(\omega)+\mathcal R\,G(\omega)=0$ then gives
$F(-\omega^*)+\overline{\mathcal R}\,G(-\omega^*)=0$, so $-\omega^*$ solves the same equation
exactly when $\mathcal R(-\omega^*)=\overline{\mathcal R(\omega)}$. For a *constant* reflectivity
that condition is $\overline{\mathcal R}=\mathcal R$, and everything below is that case. It is not
the general condition: a delayed response $\mathcal R(\omega)=r_0e^{i\omega\tau}$ with real $r_0$
and $\tau$ is complex at every real frequency and satisfies it exactly, as does any $\mathcal R$
with real Taylor coefficients in $i\omega$. Reality is sufficient and not necessary.
To test this, take Leaver's continued fraction $F(\omega)$ built on the ingoing horizon branch
$(r-1)^{-i\omega}$ and the corresponding $G(\omega)$ on the outgoing branch $(r-1)^{+i\omega}$. A
reflective horizon imposes $\psi\sim(r-1)^{-i\omega}+\mathcal R\,(r-1)^{+i\omega}$, so the
quasinormal condition becomes $F(\omega)+\mathcal R\,G(\omega)=0$, reducing to Leaver's at $\mathcal
R=0$, with linear response $d\omega/d\mathcal R=-G(\omega_0)/F'(\omega_0)$. That expression is
presented as the derivative of a stated eigenvalue condition and not as a certified physical
amplitude: rescaling $F$ and $G$ independently leaves their zeros, and so the separate spectra,
unchanged while changing $-G/F'$, so fixing it needs normalised connection amplitudes or a Wronskian
derivation that we do not supply.

For the fundamental $\ell=2$ mode, $M\omega_0=0.373672-0.088962i$ and
$M\,d\omega/d\mathcal R=-7.805\times10^{-2}+1.613\times10^{-1}i$, reproduced by finite differences at
$\mathcal R=10^{-5}$, $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-3}$. The symmetry defect
$|\omega(\mathcal R)+\overline{\omega_{\rm mirror}(\mathcal R)}|$ is then $0$ at $\mathcal R=0.001$
and $2.8\times10^{-17}$ at $\mathcal R=0.01$, both real, against $7.167\times10^{-4}$ at
$\mathcal R=0.001i$ and $7.168\times10^{-3}$ at $\mathcal R=0.01i$, linear in the imaginary part.
Within the constant-reflectivity toy, then, reality preserves the mirror symmetry and an
imaginary part breaks it. Nothing here extends that to a frequency-dependent seam.

Two limits on what this shows. It is a check of internal consistency: the prediction and the test are
both ours, and no measurement enters. And it does not favour a reflecting horizon, which the minimal
implementation of §3.4 does not adopt; it says what a reflecting implementation would have to look
like, namely that a complex reflectivity carries a spectral signature a real one cannot, so the two
are distinguishable in principle by the spectrum alone.


**A.14 Where the two sheets can meet, and what that costs.** A.13 uses the fact that the
two wedges are spacelike separated at every pair of points. That is a statement about
exteriors, and taking it for the whole answer would be a mistake, because the question of
where an inter-sheet interaction is possible has a different answer and a sharp one.
Kruskal coordinates are conformally flat in the $(T,X)$ plane, so light cones lie at
forty-five degrees and the causal structure is Minkowski's. Writing $R=\{X>|T|\}$,
$L=\{X<-|T|\}$, $F=\{T>|X|\}$ and $P=\{T<-|X|\}$,

$$
J^{+}(R)\cap J^{+}(L)=F,\qquad J^{-}(R)\cap J^{-}(L)=P,
$$

so the black hole interior is exactly the set of events both exteriors can influence, and
the white hole interior is exactly the set that can influence both. Sampling $2\times10^4$
configurations per region, no event outside $F$ admits influence from both, and the
shortfall from unity inside $F$ is the sampling of source points rather than the geometry.

The fold's own map exchanges them. $J$ is the boost through imaginary angle $\pi$, which
in Kruskal coordinates is $(T,X)\mapsto(-T,-X)$, so $R\leftrightarrow L$ and
$F\leftrightarrow P$, checked on $2\times10^4$ points with no exception. Pairing a black
hole interior with a white hole interior is therefore not a further postulate; it is the
same map that pairs the exteriors, read in the other two regions.

Three consequences follow and one cost. An inter-sheet interaction is causally impossible
outside a horizon and causally possible inside one, which is sharper than the seam
argument and independent of the seam coefficient. Anything occurring in $F$ lies in the
causal future of both exteriors and therefore reaches neither, so even a strong
inter-sheet force is unobservable from outside: §3.4's null has a second reason behind it
that does not use A.13 at all. And the contact window is finite, the longest proper time
from horizon to singularity being $\pi M$, which is $50\,\mu$s at $3M_\odot$ and four
hours at $6.5\times10^{9}M_\odot$, scaling linearly with mass.

It is worth saying what this does not do. The image of infalling matter sits in $P$, which
is in the *past* of both exteriors, so the fold returns information to the past and not to
the future. That is not a solution of the information problem as usually posed and we do
not offer it as one. Structurally it is a consistency condition between a future boundary
and a past one, which is the shape already used in §3.3 and A.9.

The cost is the domain. These results do not all lean on the same thing, and separating them shrinks
the bill. The reciprocal law needs $P_\perp^2=1$ and positivity, so it is indifferent to the state:
five states, thermal, flux-carrying, wildly non-thermal, oscillating, and a power law with no
temperature at all, each satisfy $R(x,y)R(x,P_\perp y)=1$ to machine precision. The ninety-degree equality and the odd-multipole
statement follow from it and inherit that indifference, as does the mass-independence in the paper's §3. A.10's
reduction needs $B$. Exactly one claim in these appendices needs the KMS property, that $\alpha^2=1$
fixes the temperature, and four need the two-sided structure: that same claim, the transparent seam, the
meeting region above, and A.15's consistency condition.

Evaporation, which is what breaks KMS, therefore threatens one line rather than the
appendix. Its size is $(\kappa t_{\rm evap})^{-1}\sim M^{-2}$ in Planck units, between
$10^{-77}$ at $3M_\odot$ and $10^{-94}$ at $10^{9}M_\odot$, and the violation it produces
is linear in the departure rather than discontinuous in it, unlike A.15's condition, which
empties at any breaking whatever. We have not computed the coefficient, a physical
non-equilibrium correction being nothing like the Gaussian used to test continuity, but
the conclusion tolerates more than fifty orders of magnitude of it.

What collapse takes is different, and smallness does not repair it. A hole formed by
collapse has no $P$ and no $L$, the collapsing matter replacing them, and no bifurcation
surface. For such a hole the $F\leftrightarrow P$ statement is empty, "both sheets meet in
$F$" has one sheet, and A.10's reduction has no $B$ to sit on. Those results belong first
to the cosmological horizon, which is bifurcate, which we are inside, and on which the
fold supplies $P_\perp$ with nothing chosen, and second to eternal black holes. Everything
that needs neither $B$ nor a second sheet survives for a collapse hole, the ringdown
statements among them, since they are boundary conditions on an exterior a settled
collapse hole has. The observational programme of the paper's §3 is untouched. The
domain of this appendix was not stated before and is now.

**A.15 The singularity is the fold's second contact, and what it requires there.** A.14's map does one more thing, and it locates the fold's other contact. In Kruskal coordinates the curvature singularity is the hyperbola
$T^2-X^2=1$, whose future branch bounds $F$ and whose past branch bounds $P$; since $J$
is $(T,X)\mapsto(-T,-X)$ it exchanges the two branches, verified on $2\times10^4$ points
of the locus. So under the fold the black
hole's future singularity and the white hole's past singularity are one locus, and the
fold has exactly two places where the sheets touch: the bifurcation surface and the
singularity. Only the first has been worked out here. A.14's domain caveat applies with
full force: a hole formed by collapse has no past singularity to be identified with, so
for an astrophysical black hole this section says nothing.

That changes the character of the interior boundary condition without resolving anything.
A single-sheet theory must say what happens at the singularity as a terminal condition.
The fold instead requires the data on that locus to be compatible with its own image under
$J$, which is a self-consistency requirement of the kind §3.3 already uses, and the
interior counterpart of $\alpha^2=1$ being the smoothness condition at $B$. It does not
resolve the singularity: the curvature still diverges there in both sheets, and
identifying two divergences does not remove them.

The requirement does have solutions, and counting them is finite work. Data leaves the
past branch, crosses under the evolution $U$, and reaches the future branch, where $J$
returns it, so consistency reads $Ux=Jx$, equivalently $(JU)x=x$ since $J^2=1$. If the
evolution is covariant under the fold's map, $JUJ=U^{-1}$, then $(JU)^2=1$ and the
condition becomes an eigenvalue problem for an involution. Built explicitly on a
$2n$-dimensional phase space with $J$ the antisymplectic involution $(q,p)\mapsto(q,-p)$
and $U=\exp A$ with $A$ anticommuting with $J$ inside $\mathfrak{sp}(2n)$, which is an
ordinary kinetic-plus-potential system: $(JU)^2=1$ to $2\times10^{-15}$, the spectrum is
$\pm1$ with no imaginary part, and the $+1$ eigenspace has dimension exactly $n$.

That dimension is forced, not observed. For an involution
$\operatorname{tr}M=\dim(+1)-\dim(-1)$, so the split is even exactly when
$\operatorname{tr}(JU)=0$. Since $A$ anticommutes with $J$, $A^2$ is block diagonal with
blocks $bc$ and $cb$; the diagonal blocks of $\exp A$ are the same series in each; and
$bc$ and $cb$ share a spectrum, so the two traces cancel. It is the identity
$\operatorname{tr}f(XY)=\operatorname{tr}f(YX)$ and nothing more. Checked at $n=2,5,9,16$
over two hundred draws apiece, $|\operatorname{tr}(JU)|<5\times10^{-15}$ with the
dimension $n$ every time, and it remains $n$ when $b$ and $c$ are not symmetric, so the
count comes from $A$ anticommuting with $J$ rather than from the mechanics placed inside
it. The condition
is therefore neither empty nor over-determined. It keeps exactly half the data, through
the projector $(1+JU)/2$.

That projector has the form of the Keldysh split §2.3 already uses,
$\Phi_c=(\Phi+\Theta\Phi)/2$, with the loop's involution standing where $\Theta$ stands;
the two differ by the evolution between the branches, and we record the parallel rather
than claim an identification. What the interior wants is a projection and not a terminal
condition, and the projection is the even sector of a $\mathbb{Z}_2$, as at $B$.

The covariance is load-bearing and not decorative. Adding to $A$ a piece that commutes
with $J$ instead of anticommuting, with strength $\epsilon$, destroys the involution and
the solution space with it: the kernel has dimension $n$ at $\epsilon=0$ and is empty at
$\epsilon=10^{-6}$ and above, with the smallest singular value of $JU-1$ bounded away from
zero rather than nearly degenerate. The covariance at issue is not $T$ but $\Theta$, which
is CPT, so it comes from a theorem and is untouched by $T$ violation in the kaon system.
The interior condition has solutions because CPT holds and would have none otherwise,
which puts weight on that assumption in a second place independent of §2's.

One reading of that projector should be checked rather than assumed, because it is the
natural one and it is wrong in general. A projection that keeps half the data invites being
read as a measurement, with the singularity collapsing the state onto its even sector. That
requires the projector to be self-adjoint, and $(1+JU)/2$ is not: $U$ is symplectic rather
than symmetric, $JU$ inherits that, and the projector comes out idempotent but oblique, with
range and kernel that are not orthogonal complements. An oblique projection carries no Born
rule and is not a measurement.

It becomes one in exactly one limit. The obliquity, measured as the departure of the
principal angles from orthogonality, is $0.9^\circ$ at evolution parameter $0.01$ and grows
to $82^\circ$ at $2$, vanishing only as the
evolution between the branches does. So the collapse reading is exact in the constraint
formulation and nowhere else. That is §2.5's commitment again, arriving from a fourth
direction and independently of the three recorded there.

All of this is linear, classical and finite-dimensional. An interacting field theory is
not a $12\times12$ matrix, and nothing here shows the count survives that.

One compatibility question belongs here, because it has a sharp answer. The condition
above keeps the $+1$ eigenspace of $JU$, and A.10 records that $\Theta$-invariance keeps
the $+1$ eigenspace of $\Theta$. Whether one state can satisfy both turns entirely on how
the sheet swap inside $\Theta$ sits against the time reversal in $J$, and measuring that
rather than assuming it gives three cases. If the two anticommute, the common sector is
empty at every separation including zero. If the swap is trivial, the sector is full. If
the swap *is* the time reversal, the sector is full at zero separation and empty otherwise.

A.14 settles which case applies. The fold's map is $(T,X)\mapsto(-T,-X)$, effecting
$R\leftrightarrow L$ and $F\leftrightarrow P$ together, so the swap is not an independent
involution standing alongside the time reversal but the same map acting on the wedges.
That is the third case, and the two conditions therefore share a sector exactly when there
is no evolution between the branches: the Wheeler-DeWitt situation, where the state solves
a constraint and there is no $t$ to evolve along.

This is the third calculation to reach §2.5's requirement, and it is recorded there
rather than argued again here. The conflict is not general. It was a feature of a model in which the swap and the time reversal were independent and anticommuting, a choice made
without examining it, and one A.14 had already excluded.

**A.16 Whether the fold can dispense with the Hawking temperature.** Identifying the two singular
branches invites the question of whether matter may transit the locus instead of being radiated
away, making the Hawking temperature dispensable. It may not, and the obstruction is the fold's own.
A.10 shows that in the Bunch-Davies state $\alpha^2=1$ can be *read as* $W(t-i\beta)=W(t)$, the KMS
condition, at the primitive period $\beta=2\pi/\kappa$ and, as A.10 now records, at its odd
multiples. The reading is what a non-thermal state costs, and not the identity: $\alpha^2=1$ holds
because $J$ and $P_\perp$ are commuting involutions, which is a statement about the geometry and is
true whatever state the field is in. The involution itself does not break, which reopens rather than
closes the question. In frequency space that is detailed balance, $\widetilde
W(-\omega)=e^{-\beta\omega}\widetilde W(\omega)$, and a departure from the thermal occupation
violates it at first order with no tolerant window: a fractional departure $10^{-4}$ gives a
violation $8.55\times10^{-5}$, and $10^{-2}$ gives $8.55\times10^{-3}$. So a state that departs from the thermal
occupation loses the detailed-balance reading quickly. What it does not lose is $\alpha^2=1$. The
fold's involution property therefore does not by itself make the Hawking temperature compulsory, and
the argument that it did is withdrawn. Whether the temperature can actually be dispensed with, with
the locus absorbing what would have been radiated, is left open here and turns on A.16's separate
domain result rather than on this one.

The flux is a separate matter and falls outside the domain rather than being settled. An
equilibrium state at $T_H$ is KMS and carries no net flux; an evaporating hole sits in a
state that is not KMS in Killing time, so $\alpha^2=1$ fails for it and A.10 to A.14 do
not apply. That is the same boundary this appendix records for collapse holes, reached
from a different direction. And the transit cannot substitute for evaporation in any case,
since the image of infalling matter lies in $P$, in the past of both exteriors, while
evaporation is a statement about the future. The two answer different questions.

What that leaves is a sharper statement of domain than we had, and it is narrower than it
may look. The restriction bites on the KMS-dependent claim and on nothing else: A.10's
audit of what each result leans on finds exactly one that needs KMS, namely
$\alpha^2=1$ fixing the temperature, which is this appendix's subject. The cosmological horizon is in equilibrium and genuinely KMS, and so is an eternal hole in the Hartle-Hawking state, so that claim holds without qualification on both. What it does not cover is a hole that formed and will evaporate. Extending to astrophysical holes needs the departure of their state
from equilibrium, of order $(\kappa t_{\rm evap})^{-1}$, to degrade the predictions
smoothly rather than void them. That calculation would widen the domain and is not done
here.




### The matter rule, neutrino masses and the conditional decay scale

We use BFT's gravitational-production state and radiation history [12,13]. In the adopted small-Weyl-coupling branch, matching the observed dark-matter abundance gives $M_1=4.916\times10^8$ GeV. This number is conditional on that production model and state selection.

The sterile-species $\mathbb Z_2$ is an additional matter-theory assumption. The quotient admits a nontrivial sign bundle, but the bundle pulls back trivially to the simply connected physical cover. We have not constructed a continuation from the Euclidean boundary data to an interacting cover action and measure that enforce the same species rule. The phenomenology therefore retains BFT's imposed stabilisation rule.

When that rule is exact, the sterile particle cannot decay. Its forbidden Yukawa column makes the light-neutrino mass matrix rank at most two in the stated seesaw approximation. The two measured oscillation splittings then leave one light neutrino massless. For normal ordering and the oscillation inputs used here, $\Sigma m_\nu=58.78\pm0.32$ meV, with the independent input errors propagated at first order. The ordering is specified separately; exact stabilisation alone does not choose it.

The double-beta interval in the paper's §3.3 spans the remaining unknown Majorana phase. The fold does not select a point within that interval.

A concrete weakly broken implementation adds a small gauge-invariant Yukawa column $-y_\alpha\overline L_\alpha\widetilde H N_{1R}+{\rm h.c.}$, with the other two sterile neutrinos heavier and no other open $N_1$ decay channel. This is an additional matter interaction, not a consequence of the fold. Write $q=y^\dagger y$ and $H^0=(v+h+iG^0)/\sqrt2$, with $v=246.22$ GeV. At tree level and $M_1\gg m_W,m_Z,m_h$ [32],

$$
\Gamma_0=\frac{qM_1}{8\pi},\qquad
{\rm Br}(h\nu):{\rm Br}(Z\nu):{\rm Br}(W\ell)=1:1:2,
$$

where the charged channel includes both charges. A pure Higgs-neutrino branching assignment is therefore unavailable in this example. The same column adds $\delta m_\nu=-v^2yy^T/(2M_1)$. Relative to the rank-two tree-level baseline, its smallest mass obeys

$$
m_{\min}\leq\|\delta m_\nu\|_2
=\frac{4\pi v^2\hbar}{M_1^2\tau},\qquad \tau=\hbar/\Gamma_0.
$$

Duran, Morrison and Profumo [36] computed the gamma-ray and neutrino emission from decaying ultra-heavy sterile neutrinos across generalized CPT-symmetric cosmologies, and report a reach to lifetimes near $10^{27}$ s for very-high-energy Cherenkov telescopes and $10^{30}$ s for neutrino telescopes. The example lifetime used below is inside that second window, so it is a testable value rather than an arbitrary one. For that example $\tau=10^{28}$ s, $\|y\|\simeq1.8\times10^{-30}$ and the bound is about $2.1\times10^{-55}$ eV. This bounds the added tree-level mass term; radiative masses of the baseline are a separate question. With the adopted radiation history and only this new interaction, the inverse-decay population and the decay depletion are negligible at the accuracy of the abundance benchmark (Supplement S13). Thus a long-lived decay need not spoil the abundance or the effectively massless light-neutrino result, although exact stabilisation and a nonzero decay cannot hold simultaneously.

For a cold parent at rest, the neutral hard channels have

$$
E_{\nu,b}=\frac{M_1^2-m_b^2}{2M_1}\simeq\frac{M_1}{2},\qquad b=h,Z.
$$

Borah, Das, Okada and Sarmah [37] reach the same kinematics from the other direction. They take a heavy right-handed neutrino of $440$ PeV and show its decay reproduces the KM3NeT flux at the observed $220$ PeV, reporting that the lifetime required for the best fit saturates existing gamma-ray bounds. The mass differs from $M_1$ here by ten per cent. The logical direction is what differs: they select the mass to fit the measured energy, whereas $M_1$ above is fixed by matching the dark-matter abundance in the adopted production branch, with the half-mass energy following. We claim no priority for the decay interpretation of that event, which is theirs. What the fold adds is that the scale was not chosen to land there.

The two neutral hard channels $h\nu$ and $Z\nu$ therefore both give 245.8 PeV at the computational mass benchmark, a physical scale of roughly 245 PeV. Electroweak radiation and boson decay produce a continuum and change the endpoint weight at this energy [33]. Halo velocities, extragalactic redshift, propagation and detector response must also be included to predict a measured spectrum. Neither that spectrum nor an event rate is calculated here. Two consequences of the $1:1:2$ ratio are
worth separating, because one closes a hope and the other opens a test.

Flavour cannot do the assigning that the paper's §3.2 falsifier needs. The channel ratio is
flavour-blind: all three channels carry the same $|y_\alpha|^2$, and the $1:1:2$ comes from
gauge and Higgs structure rather than from the Yukawa column, which stays free. Since the
oscillation-averaged transfer is linear, the Earth-side compositions the decay can reach
are exactly the convex hull of the images of pure-$e$, pure-$\mu$ and pure-$\tau$ sources,
which is the image of the whole source simplex. Every astrophysical source composition is
itself a point of that simplex, so the decay reproduces any of them by choice of Yukawa
column, and pion-decay, muon-damped, neutron-decay and equal-flavour sources are all
reachable. An Earth-side flavour measurement therefore
cannot separate this component from an astrophysical population, and the paper's §3.2 directional requirement stands undiminished.

What the ratio does fix is the split between the hard line and everything else, and that
is a discriminant. Of four decays one gives $h\nu$ and one $Z\nu$, both feeding the line at
$M_1/2$, while two give $W\ell$, whose charged lepton never reaches Earth and whose
products land in the continuum. The line-to-continuum normalisation is therefore set by
tree-level structure and is independent of the free Yukawa column, where an astrophysical
population has no reason to hold a fixed ratio between a line and its own continuum.
How visible that is can be settled without the fragmentation calculation, by asking how
much the answer depends on it. Write the endpoint continuum as $N_{\rm tot}(n+1)(1-x)^n$
with $x=2E/M_1$, so the continuum inside a window $\delta$ below the endpoint is
$N_{\rm tot}\delta^{\,n+1}$ while the line contributes $0.5$ regardless. At the thirty per cent resolution of the paper's §3.2 and a cascade multiplicity of $10^2$ to $10^3$, the line is a
feature of order tens of per cent of the endpoint continuum for a fall-off $n=3$ to $5$,
and is swamped at $n=2$. Across a grid spanning
both unknowns it clears a fifth of the local continuum at $81$ per cent of the grid points at
that resolution and at $50$ per cent at fifty per cent resolution. Those are fractions of points
on a scan with no measure on it, not probabilities and not a detector forecast. The claim they
support is only that the $1:1:2$ structure fixes the tree-level neutral-channel yield at $0.5$
per decay; what fraction of that is observable at an endpoint needs electroweak showering,
propagation and detector response, none of which is done here. Visibility is real in the
tree-level sense and genuinely conditional on everything downstream of it.

The part that needs nothing is the lock. That $0.5$ is fixed by the $1:1:2$ tree-level
ratio and by nothing else: not the Yukawa column, not the lifetime, not the halo profile,
not the fragmentation. The prediction is therefore not a ratio but a constraint, that the
line strength and the decay rate are tied at a fixed fraction, two hard neutrinos per four
decays. An astrophysical population has no reason to tie a line-like feature to its own
continuum normalisation at any fixed value, which is what makes this a discriminant where
flavour is not. It is a population-level statement and not a per-event one,
since measuring a lock requires both the line and the continuum it is locked to, so it belongs with the paper's §3.2 directional requirement rather than with the single-event test. The two
are the same regime and cost the same order of exposure, which is the useful part: a
population that can place the events on the sky can also weigh the line against its own
continuum, and the two discriminants come together or not at all. KM3NeT's broad reconstructed event-energy interval [30] is not evidence for this decay assignment; no likelihood preference over an astrophysical population is claimed.

The distinction is experimentally useful. A spectrum and flux calculated for a specified lifetime and flavour direction can be tested with neutrino and photon observations. A channel exclusion would constrain that implementation, while exact stabilisation predicts no decay signal. Neither observation would by itself establish or reject the entire two-copy geometry.
